# CLAUDE.md

Classic Arkanoid (Breakout) game. Unity `6000.5.6f1`, Universal Render Pipeline, new Input System.

Gameplay is strictly 2D (2D physics on the XY plane) but the visuals are 3D: bricks and paddle are box meshes, the ball is a sphere, all with URP Lit materials (`Assets/Materials`), lit by the scene's directional light in front of a shadow-catching `Backdrop` box whose front face stands `ScreenChange.FogWall` (0.55) behind the playing plane — the same gap the menu's backdrop keeps, wearing the same matte fog material (`MenuBackdrop.mat`, `ScreenChange.FogColor`), because a round stands in the same fog the menu does (see below). The light faces the playfield head-on horizontally and tilts down by `ArkanoidSetup.LightPitch` (30°). Nothing here casts onto a floor — every shadow lands on a surface *behind* its caster — so the shadow's offset from the object is `gap × tan(pitch)`: a steeper, "higher" light smears the shadow further down the backdrop, and a shallower one tucks it back in behind the object. 30° keeps shadows readable as a depth cue without the long drop the earlier 50° gave. The camera is perspective (FOV 50 at z −13.5, framing-equivalent to the old orthographic size 6). Blocks wear one of nine shared **block materials** (`Assets/Materials/Block*.mat`), since what a block is made of is what says how hard it is (see "A block is a shape and a material") — and, for a material that has any variety authored for it, a per-instance override on top of that saying which pressing of the substance this particular block came out of (see "A material varies from block to block"). The crack overlay stays a `SpriteRenderer` child sitting just in front of the box's face. A generated tileable brick-masonry texture (`Assets/Textures/BrickWall.png`, grayscale so the material tint colors it) survives from the walled playfield in the menu's block lettering; the `WallSide`/`WallTop` materials and meshes it was made for do not stand in the scene any more.

## The playfield is the frame

**A round runs the whole width of the window**, exactly as the menu screens always have: there are no walls standing in the picture, only three invisible `Border`s laid along the left, right and top edges of what the camera actually sees, so the ball ricochets off the edge of the screen in a burst of sparks. Nothing about this can be authored — a frame is only known once there is a window, and it changes when the window does — so `Playfield` (on the room's root, which `GameManager.playfieldObjects` switches off for the menu) fits it all in `OnEnable` and again whenever the window is resized: the borders, the `Backdrop`'s size (measured at its *own* z, since it stands further back than the room and has a wider frame to cover, plus `BackdropOverhang` so its edge is never seen), the two fog banks' sizes (each at its own depth, the same way), and how far the paddle may travel (`Paddle.FitTo`, which keeps the paddle's measured body inside the frame). `GameManager.BuildLevel` reads `Playfield.HalfWidth` back and cuts the brick grid to as many whole slots as fit with `BrickEdgeMargin` (0.9) clear either side — 12 columns at 16:9 where the walled field held 8. Only the width is worked out that way: the camera's field of view is vertical, so the frame is the same height whatever the window's shape and the rows stay at the `y0` 4.6 they were authored at. The frame is measured as extents and centred on *the room* rather than on the camera, since the view travels here from the menu with both rooms standing.

The score and lives readouts moved to the **bottom** corners with the widening: the field reaches the top corners now, and the strip under the paddle is the one part of the screen a round has nothing standing in.

### Rubble is worth catching

**Every chunk a brick breaks into can be caught on the paddle**, for `GameManager.DebrisPoints` (10) each, so breaking a brick is only half the score in it. A brick throws 6–9 fragments and pays 100–500, which is the balance: a whole brick's rubble gathered is worth about as much again as breaking it was, and none of it is free — the rubble falls where the brick stood and the paddle has to leave the ball to be under it. That tension is the point of the feature. It is also why the rubble now rains the full height of the field rather than shrinking away in the air over the bricks: a fragment that can be caught has to survive the whole fall, or it would wink out on its way to a paddle already moving for it (`Debris`, in Layout, for how a fragment's two lives differ).

Nothing about it touches the rally. Fragments carry no colliders — they never did — so the catch is `Debris` testing itself against the paddle's drawn body in its own `Update`, and the ball's 2D physics never learns any of it happened. Only a round in play pays: `GameManager.Catcher` is null on the menu, whose slabs shatter through the same `Debris.Spawn`, and `OnDebrisCaught` refuses a chunk that lands after the round is over, since rubble goes on falling over an end screen and over name entry.

**The round stands in the same fog the menu does**, because a round is a continuation of the menu screens rather than a different place. Its `Backdrop` is the same wall at the same gap — front face at `ScreenChange.FogWall`, wearing the shared `MenuBackdrop.mat` so the two rooms' murk can never drift apart — and two banks of drifting haze (`PlayfieldFogFar`/`PlayfieldFogNear`, the same `MenuFog` component) hang in front of it. They are root scenery like the `Backdrop`, standing while the room's colliders are switched off for the menu, so the weather is already there when the camera travels in; `Playfield` holds their reins (`fogBanks`) and sizes each to the frame at its own depth. Their depths are 0.46 and 0.37 rather than the menu's 0.42 and 0.3: the thickest thing on the menu's plane is an option arrow at a quarter of a unit of half-depth, but a brick carries 0.3, so the menu's near bank would lie exactly on every brick's back face.

The menu's paddle and ball are scaled by `ArkanoidSetup.MenuPlayScale` — the ratio of the two planes' depths from the camera (9.8 / 13.5) — because the menu's plane stands nearer the camera and the same world size looked bigger there. `MenuBallSpeed` is the round's own `PlayfieldBallSpeed` (8, what `Ball.cs` authors as its default) scaled by the same ratio, so the menu's ball crosses its screen at the same rate a round's crosses its own rather than merely matching it in world units. It was a gentler 6.5 for a while, on the theory that the menu is steered rather than fought, and the seam showed — a ball that picks up speed on the way into a round says the two screens are two different games. `Ball.RestOffset` measures the paddle it sits on and its own radius rather than using a fixed height, so the smaller pair doesn't float apart.

### The paddle can be a rocket

**SPACE is a thrust.** Held with an arrow, it drives the paddle up to `Paddle.BoostTopSpeed` (3.5) times the one speed it has ever had, over `BoostTime` (0.9 of a second), and it falls back to cruising over `CoastTime` (0.3) once the thrust is let go of — or held with no arrow to point it, which is the same thing. Three and a half times is chosen so the far side of the field is suddenly reachable: a ball that had beaten the paddle can be caught, at a price. The two numbers go together — a higher top speed wants a longer climb, or the extra speed arrives before anyone has decided to want it, and the climb is most of a second precisely so that it is felt as a climb.

The drive is kept as a **signed multiple of cruising speed** rather than as units a second, because that is the number every rule about it is written in: 1 is the paddle the player already knows, anything above it is borrowed, and the crash below reads it straight. Inside cruising speed the paddle answers the keys exactly as it always did — instantly, with nothing carried over — so a player who never touches SPACE is playing the old game.

The thrust builds **on top of** cruising rather than up to it: pressed from a standstill, or against the way the paddle is already going, the drive is put at cruising speed the way the arrow points and the rocket takes it from there. Spooling up from nothing was the first version, and it made the opening of every boost *slower* than the arrow alone would have been — a paddle asked to hurry must not be worse off for having been asked.

Two things it deliberately is not:

- **Not a second speed setting.** Most of a second of spool-up means the speed is committed to before it is had, and the coast down means it cannot be cancelled the moment it stops being wanted. Where the paddle will be in half a second is the thing being wagered, and that is the whole of the mechanic.
- **Not free at the edge.** Running out of field spends the drive entirely — what stopped the paddle is a wall — and a paddle jammed against the edge with the arrow still pushing it there builds no thrust at all, so holding a boost into the wall rings the room once rather than once a spool-up. Leaving the edge costs a fresh spool-up: a rocket does not carry speed it never got to use.

SPACE serves the ball as well, and the two never argue, because they read different halves of the same key: the launch takes the press (`wasPressedThisFrame`) and the thrust takes the hold. The same key that serves the ball drives the paddle out from under it. The menu's paddle carries the same component, so a menu ball can be chased down at three times the speed too — and the menu's room shakes for the same reason a round's does.

**A crash rings the room, as hard as it was.** `Paddle` hands `ViewShake.Shake` a force of `InverseLerp(1, BoostTopSpeed, |drive|)`, so a paddle merely cruising into the edge asks for nothing (a cruising bump measures force 0.00 and no shake is started at all) and a fully spooled one asks for everything (measured at the earlier 3× top speed: drive 3.00, force 1.00) — the crash is proportional to what was carrying the paddle over the speed it is allowed for free.

**The thrust is visible, and it is the drive that is visible.** A paddle at three and a half times its own speed looks exactly like a paddle, only sooner somewhere else, so `JetTrail` hangs an exhaust off its trailing edge whenever the drive is over 1 — under thrust or coasting a boost off, since a paddle carrying borrowed speed is a paddle with the exhaust still in it. That is the same 1.0 the crash is measured against, so what the player can see is exactly what the game is reading: the flame thickens as the drive climbs, thins as it bleeds away, and is gone the instant the paddle is back to its own speed. A paddle jammed against the edge of the frame throws nothing, however hard the keys are pushed — the engine is not running, and `Drift` is what says so.

It is made of two kinds of piece, like a `Ricochet`: the **plume** carries the shape and the **embers** carry the detail, both plain cubes moved by hand with no physics components and nothing authored, which is how every cosmetic in this game is built. Three things about it are worth knowing, because each replaced an obvious version that was wrong:

- **A plume is the swept path, not a puff.** Each one spans from where the nozzle was last frame to where it is now, so consecutive frames' pieces abut end to end and the ribbon is continuous at any frame rate. A puff per frame was the first version, and it read as a ribbon at sixty frames a second and as a dotted line at the ten a backgrounded editor manages — a trail whose thickness was a fact about the machine rather than about the paddle.
- **The flame's length is chosen and its pieces' lifetime is derived**, not the other way round. The ribbon is the ground the nozzle covers while a piece is alive, so a fixed lifetime gives a flame whose length *is* the paddle's speed: a yard of it while spooling up and half the field at the top of the thrust. Length is the thing worth choosing, so it is chosen — in paddle heights, `3.5` to `8` of them by strength, so the menu's scaled-down paddle gets a scaled-down flame — and `life = length / speed` falls out of it.
- **Everything is measured off the paddle's drawn body**, never assumed: the nozzle is its trailing edge, the flame's thickness and the embers' size are fractions of its height. The menu's paddle is a scaled-down copy of the round's and carries the same component, so the menu's ball can be chased down at three and a half times the speed with a smaller rocket.

A piece is tinted **every frame** rather than once at birth (`Hot` → `Flame` → `Ash`), which is the one place in the game where a thing's colour is the whole point of it: white at the nozzle, orange down the ribbon, all but out at the tail. An ember starts at the beginning of that cooling and has its whole fall to get through it; a plume starts white-hot so the end attached to the paddle is the bright end. They are matte (`_Smoothness` 0) because the paddle's own material is glossy and a highlight sliding across a flame gives away that it is a box, and they cast no shadow, because every shadow on either screen lands on the backdrop and a flame's would be a dark smear chasing the paddle across the fog.

`ViewShake` is the shake, and it shakes **the camera**: everything in both rooms is authored in world space and the view is the only thing that ever moves between them, so throwing the view is the one way to shake the scene without touching a single thing standing in it. A knock throws it `MaxThrow` (0.28 of a unit, a shade over 2% of the 12-unit-tall frame) at full force, wobbling at two rates that are not multiples of each other (`RateX` 47, `RateY` 31 radians a second, less of it vertically than horizontally since the blow was delivered sideways) with a fresh random phase per knock, dying away over `Duration` (0.32s) on a squared falloff so it is loudest at the moment of impact. A knock landing on a view that is still ringing takes it over rather than adding to it, since two summed would throw the picture further than the hardest single hit is allowed to.

It is **runtime-only and never authored**: the first knock adds the component to whatever `Camera.main` is, so neither the scene nor `ArkanoidSetup` has to know the mechanic exists. The offset it applied is taken back off before the next one is worked out, so the **rest position is read back out of the transform rather than remembered** — which is what lets `GameManager.MoveViewTo` go on writing the camera's x directly (the travel from the menu into a round is exactly that) without the two fighting over the same number. When a knock is spent the view has just been handed back exactly where it was found and the component switches itself off, so a still view costs nothing a frame; the next knock switches it back on. Getting that wrong is the one way this can leave a mark: an earlier version could never reach its own spent branch, which left `strength` standing, so the next gentle bump would have been shaken as hard as the loudest one before it.

### The bounce can be twisted

**A moving paddle drags the bounce with it.** The arcade bounce has always read the hit's distance from the paddle's middle, as a fraction of its half-width, straight off as the tangent of the angle the ball leaves at; now `Paddle.Drift` — which way the paddle was travelling over the last frame, as −1, 0 or 1 — is added to that fraction through `Ball.TwistReach` (0.35) before it is used. So a dead-centre hit off a paddle sliding right leaves at about 19° off vertical where a still paddle would have sent the ball straight up, and a ball that arrived in the wrong place can be *aimed* by shoving the paddle under it rather than only by meeting it in the right spot. That is the point of the feature: where the ball struck stays the louder half of where it goes, and the twist is what the player adds to it.

The sum is clamped back into the ±1 the offset alone spans, which is what keeps every rule written downstream of the paddle honest: `Ball.MinAngle` justifies itself by name on a paddle bounce never coming off flatter than 45°, and a twist that could push the tangent past 1 would quietly break that. The clamp is also what gives the twist its feel — a ball caught out at the very end of the paddle cannot be twisted any wider, only hauled back towards the middle, so the widest angles stay the reward for reaching the ball with the paddle's tip.

`Drift` is read off whether the clamp let the frame's move through rather than off the key that is held, because the two part company exactly where it matters: a paddle jammed against the edge of the field with the key still down has stopped, and a stopped paddle has no twist to give. Only flat-top hits are twisted; the rounded corners keep the engine's reflection off the curve, as they always have, so the twist lives entirely inside the arcade override. The menu's paddle carries the same component, so the menu's ball can be twisted onto an option arrow the same way.

**The drag is also scuffed into the ball, and that is the half of it the player can see.** `Ball.spin` holds it as a signed fraction of a full one — the paddle's `Drift` at the moment of the hit, whatever the aim did with it — and it is spent over the second that follows as two things read off the one number, so the roll on screen and the bend in the flight can never disagree:

- **The ball turns** (`RollSpeed`, 1200°/s at the instant of the hit, `RollSpeed / SpinDecay` = better than six full turns before it is spent; three and a third turns a second is about as fast as four panels can come round while still reading as panels rather than a flicker). The ball was authored with its rotation frozen, and a plain sphere turning is a sphere standing still, so it now wears four panels (`Assets/Textures/BallPanels.png`) and its rotation is unfrozen. Neither touches the rally: the collider is a circle, so its rotation is not a fact any contact can read, and `FixedUpdate` writes the angular velocity itself every step rather than leaving it to the engine — which also means friction against a face can never set the ball turning behind the mechanic's back.
- **The flight bends** (`CurveRate`, 65°/s at full spin) — the "air" the ball is travelling through. The heading is rotated a little every step rather than pushed by a force, since the ball's speed is renormalized to `speed` every step anyway and a force would only be argued with; the result is a circular arc. In the first second after a full-tilt hit, while most of the spin is still on the ball, that is around 50° of the ball's line — an arc nobody has to be told about.

**No ball in play is ever still.** Three things see to it, and each was added because the ball was caught not turning somewhere:

- **Every contact nicks it.** Whatever was hit, the contact scrubs a little off the ball's turn and puts a little random back on (`BounceScrub` 0.85, `BounceNick` ±0.14, its magnitude drawn from the top half of that range by `NickFloor` so a bounce cannot leave the ball still). Random, because a bounce's real spin depends on where on the ball the face caught it, which 2D physics does not model and a circle collider could not report.
- **The serve leaves with a fixed spin to the right** (`LaunchSpin`, a whole nick's worth). A ball is rolled off the paddle rather than let go of in mid-air, so it has no business leaving perfectly still — and a freshly served ball was the last one in the game that did. It is deliberately *not* random, for the reason `LaunchAngle` is not: the waiting ball sits right of the paddle's middle because that is where the angle it will leave at comes from, and it now leaves rolling that way too — right of centre, off to the right, turning to the right, the same fact told three times. A random serve spin put a small unasked-for curve on the very first shot, in a direction the player had no way to read off the screen.
- **The turn is held off zero from underneath** (`MinSpin` 0.06, so never below `MinSpin × RollSpeed` = 72°/s). A nick can land against the spin the ball already had and very nearly cancel it, and a long flight with nothing struck bleeds most of the way to nothing on its own; both left the ball all but static. Only the *direction* of a ball's turn is ever in doubt now. It costs a permanent, tiny curve — under 4° a second — which is a ball that never flies quite straight, and that is nearer the truth than one that does.

A nick is deliberately far too small to aim with: it carries a fifth of the bend a full spin does, so the paddle's drag is worth five bounces and stays the only thing that moves a ball's line. Measured across 76 seconds of flight, the ball turns at 72°/s at the floor and 95°/s through the middle of a rally, spiking past 1000°/s off a full-tilt paddle catch — and it was never once measured still.

The spin **bleeds** rather than counting down (`SpinDecay` 0.5 a second, so half of what is left goes every second and a half). It was a linear countdown first, on the reasoning that a spin ought to *end* rather than trail off for ever — and then a ball with no turn at all on it turned out to be the thing that looked wrong. The tail is the point now, and the nicks and the floor keep it fed.

The bend goes in **ahead** of `MinAngle`, so the floor on the ball's angle stays the last word — a curve that has flattened the ball too far is steepened back like any other flat ball. That floor is what stops a strong curve running away, and it is doing real work rather than sitting idle: over 76 seconds of measured flight the ball was riding it 7% of the time. If rallies ever start reading as flat, `CurveRate` is the dial, not `MinAngle`.

`SpinHandedness` decides which way the bend goes, and the choice is deliberately not the aerodynamic one. **A ball scuffed to the right rolls clockwise and arcs clockwise** — it rolls *into* its own turn, the way a ball running round the inside of a bowl does, so the roll the player sees and the curve they get are the same fact twice, and the curve carries on the way the shove aimed. True Magnus lift on that spin bends the other way, which would have the ball undo the shove the player just aimed with. Flipping that one constant is the whole of that change if the honest version is ever wanted.

A paddle hit **adds to** the spin the ball arrived with rather than replacing it (`ImpactScrub` 0.5: half the old turn, plus the drag, clamped). Replacing it was the first version, and a ball caught on a paddle standing still stopped dead mid-roll, which reads as the mechanic being switched off rather than as a ball being caught. Measured across a catch: a ball arriving at 0.13 with the paddle dragging −1 came off at −0.94, where replacement would have given exactly −1. It also means a rally can build a spin up over several hits. Spin is cleared on `AttachTo` along with everything else a fresh serve owes nothing to — and the angular velocity with it, because an attached ball is kinematic and `FixedUpdate` is not looking at it, so a ball put back on the paddle mid-spin would sit there spinning.

The paddle has rounded corners: a rounded-rectangle prism mesh (`Assets/Meshes/Paddle.asset`, 2 × 0.4 × 0.5, corner radius 0.15, authored at final size so the transform is unit-scale) paired with a `BoxCollider2D` shrunk by the radius on every side (1.7 × 0.1) plus `edgeRadius: 0.15` — exactly the same rounded rectangle — so the ball's bounce reflects off the corner curve's true normal. `Ball.cs` applies its arcade paddle-bounce override only when the contact normal is straight up (flat-top hits); corner hits keep the engine's reflection.

### A block is a shape and a material

**A block's hardness is its shape's base times its material's multiplier**, and those are two separate authored facts. The shape is a prefab (`baseHardness` on `Brick`), the material is one of nine shared assets, and a level picks them independently. Colour used to carry the whole job on its own — one white `Brick.mat` tinted per row, with `rowHardness` written beside `rowColors` and nothing keeping the two in step. The material *is* the look now, so how many hits a block owes the player is something they read off what it is made of.

**The shapes, and their base hardness.** Each prefab's 2D collider matches its visual outline exactly, so the ball reflects off the shape's true contact normal — a shape is physics here, not trim. Full-slot slabs are base 2; the half-width and round blocks are base 1, since small blocks are flimsy and there are two of them in every slot they fill.

- `Brick.prefab` — the normal 1.5 × 0.5 box (`Assets/Meshes/BrickBox.asset` + `BoxCollider2D`). Base 2. It was a scaled *stock* cube until the blocks got a tiled grain; see "A material varies from block to block" for why the stock cube cannot carry one.
- `RoundedBrick.prefab` — full-size with corner radius **0.2**: a rounded-rectangle prism mesh (`Assets/Meshes/BrickRounded.asset`, built by the same `BuildRoundedPrismMesh` as the paddle, unit-scale transform) with a 1.1 × 0.1 `BoxCollider2D` + `edgeRadius: 0.2`, so corner hits reflect off the curve. Base 2. The radius was 0.12 for a long time, which left the top face 84% flat: the corners were always real physics and the ball almost never found them. The ceiling is `BrickHeight / 2` = 0.25, at which the ends are full semicircles and the block is a stadium; 0.2 is plainly curved while the face is still a face.
- `HalfBrick.prefab` — a half-width 0.68 × 0.5 box (`Assets/Meshes/HalfBrickBox.asset`, the same world-UV builder at its own width); two of them plus the level's 0.14 gap fill one normal slot (2 × 0.68 + 0.14 = 1.5). Base 1.
- `RoundBrick.prefab` — a 0.5-diameter sphere (stock sphere at scale 0.5) with a `CircleCollider2D` (radius 0.5 × scale = 0.25 world), so hits deflect along the circle's radial normal. Base 1. Its crack overlay child is scaled 0.7 (≈ inscribed square) to stay over the circular silhouette.

So the four shapes are a deflection ladder as well as a hardness one: flat reflection, flat face with curved ends, narrow box, fully radial.

**The materials.** `BlockMaterial` is the enum, `BlockMaterials.Of` the rules, `ArkanoidSetup.BlockMaterialLooks` the looks. The enum's order is the index into `GameManager.blockMaterials`, so entries may be appended but never reshuffled.

| × | Material | Look |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | ForceField | translucent blue, lit from within — unbreakable |
| 1 | Polymer | matte near-white |
| 1 | Ceramics | chalky bone |
| 1 | Antimatter | magenta, lit — detonates its neighbours |
| 2 | Titanium | brushed grey metal |
| 2 | Crystal | translucent blue-green, very smooth |
| 3 | Inconel | warm nickel with a gold heat-tint |
| 4 | Waspaloy | dark pewter, plainly metal |
| 5 | Neutronium | returns nothing at all |

Multipliers are whole numbers throughout, because a hardness is a count of hits and the crack overlay has only two stages to spend across it — a half-step would read as noise rather than as wear. Names are one word, a real substance where one exists and a real physics term where none does, with the fancy aerospace alloys owning the engineered tiers; invented names were tried and dropped, since a player already knows titanium beats polymer and that ordering is free.

Three things about the looks were learned the hard way, and all three are facts about this scene rather than about colour:

- **A non-metal can never be darker than about 4% reflectance** — its dielectric specular floor, a good 0.22 in sRGB. Neutronium was first written as matte black with an albedo of nothing and came out a *mid grey*, lighter than the ×4 below it, standing the top of the ladder on its head. It is full metallic over near-black now, barely smooth enough to catch anything: a metal has no such floor, because its reflectance *is* its base colour. Waspaloy keeps a plain highlight precisely so this one can be the absence of one.
- **Full metallic on a dark base renders as nothing here.** A metal has no diffuse albedo and this scene has no reflection probe, so the only light it can return is a highlight it never gets. That is what Waspaloy was at first, and it is why it is dark pewter at metallic 0.8 rather than the near-black it was written as.
- **URP defaults a transparent material to its preserve-specular path**, which sets `_SrcBlend` to One and blends the block *additively* — a force field that came out a solid glowing cyan rather than a pane anything could be seen through. `_BlendModePreserveSpecular` is turned off, so ForceField and Crystal are plain alpha.

Colours are written in sRGB and converted once with `.linear` on the way to the shader, for the same reason `PowerWave`'s ramp is (see "The push can be charged").

**ForceField cannot be arithmetic.** A multiplier of zero would make `damage >= Hardness` true before the ball ever arrived and the block would break itself on spawn, so `BlockMaterialTraits.Unbreakable` is an explicit flag: `TakeDamage` refuses the hit outright and shows no wear, and `GameManager.SpawnBrick` leaves the block out of `bricksLeft` — a round that counted one would wait for ever to be won.

**Antimatter detonates, and the chain propagates.** Breaking one destroys everything touching it — an overlap box of its own bounds grown by `Brick.BlastReach` (0.2), which is over the grid's 0.14 gap and under the next block's width, so it catches exactly the ring around it, corners included, whatever shape either of them is. The blast **ignores hardness**: a Neutronium slab beside antimatter simply goes, because a block that survived the explosion next door would make placing one a calculation rather than a decision. Neighbours pay full points and throw their own rubble. A force field is immune to the blast too — unbreakable that only held against the ball would be a much smaller word. Antimatter caught in a blast detonates in turn, and `Brick.broken` is what stops two of them side by side going back and forth for ever: `Destroy` is deferred to the end of the frame, so a block already on its way out is still very much in the physics world.

Measured, from a row of `Neutronium | Antimatter | Antimatter | ForceField` with a Polymer block out of reach: hitting the first antimatter twice (base 2 × ×1) destroyed it, the ×5 Neutronium beside it and the second antimatter, left the force field standing, and never reached the distant block.

**The level is a demonstration board.** `BuildLevel` lays out every shape crossed with every material, exactly once each: a row is a shape (`GameManager.boardKinds` — Normal, Rounded, Half, Round) and a column is a material in the enum's own order, so 4 × 9 = 36 blocks and the whole of what a block can be is on screen at once. Half and Round blocks stand **one to a slot** here rather than the two they used to, because one of each is the point; being half a slot wide they sit centred with daylight either side, which is its own way of saying they are the small ones.

Nothing is cut to fit any more. The grid used to be trimmed to however many whole slots the window held (`BrickEdgeMargin`, now gone with the fields that fed it), and a board that dropped combinations on a narrow window would not be showing all of them — so the nine columns are always laid: 9 × 1.64 − 0.14 = 14.62 units, which clears the frame at any aspect from 4:3 up. `PrefabFor` falls back to the normal brick if a variant prefab is unwired, and `MaterialAsset` falls back to null — and so to whatever the prefab was wearing — if a material is not.

Two things follow from putting every material on the board at once, and both are wanted rather than tolerated. The four force fields are excluded from `bricksLeft`, so the round is won by clearing the other 32. And the antimatter column stands four antimatter blocks one above another with a ceramics and a titanium block either side, so a single hit on any of them chains the whole column and its neighbours — which is the most direct demonstration of the mechanic the board could give.

Points are still `(rows − row) × 100` by row, which now means by *shape* — 400 down to 100, top to bottom. That is left over from the graded level and no longer says much: hardness varies from 1 to 10 across a single row here, so what a block is worth and what it costs to break have come apart. Worth revisiting if this board outlives its demonstration.

### A material varies from block to block

**What a block is made of is a shared fact; which pressing of it this block came out of is not.** `BlockVariety` (one serialized entry per material on `GameManager.blockVarieties`) says how far one block of a material may drift from the next: which surface grain it was moulded with, how light or dark the batch came out, how glossy that left it, and where in the tiled grain it happens to sit. `Roll` decides all of it once, at spawn, and `Brick.SetLook` puts it on. Nothing here can touch a hardness — the rule stays the material's and the variety is only ever the look.

The problem it solves is that a shared material asset made every block wearing it pixel-wise identical, which is exactly right for a *warning* — nine looks, each one a hardness — and wrong for a *surface*: thirty-six blocks of the same grey rectangle read as a spreadsheet rather than as a wall of plastic. **Polymer is the only material with an entry so far**; a material with none wears its shared asset untouched, which is not a failure but the ordinary case (`GameManager.VarietyOf` answers null and `SetLook` is never called).

**It goes on as a `MaterialPropertyBlock`, and that costs the batching the shared materials were there to protect.** A property block opts the renderer out of the SRP batcher, so blocks of one material no longer batch together — worth paying at three dozen blocks, and worth knowing before a level holds three hundred. The alternative, `new Material(asset)` per block, hands every brick a copy to leak; a fixed set of pre-baked variant assets was the other option and was dropped because it multiplies assets by every material that ever gets a band. The comment on `Brick.SetMaterial` still describes the batching as though it held; the one on `SetLook` directly under it is what is true.

**The grain lives in a normal map, not in the albedo**, and that is the load-bearing decision. Three tileable grains are generated by `ArkanoidSetup` (`Assets/Textures/Polymer{Pebble,Stipple,Crumb}.png` plus a `*Normal.png` each, from `WriteGrainTextures`): scattered rounded caps combined by **max** rather than by sum — a sum of overlapping discs normalises down to mush where a max is a field of distinct caps, which is what moulded plastic is — drawn wrapped so the tile is seamless and a random per-block offset into it is as good as none. The albedo is near-white with a whisper of value in it and the relief is all in the normal, because grain is relief and not pigment: a near-black block whose grain lived in its albedo would have nothing left to be dark with, and the texture would vanish exactly where the block most needs it. Retuning a grain means deleting the PNGs and letting stage 91 write them again, keeping the `.meta` files so the guids stage 95 wired survive — the same way stage 79's ball texture is retuned.

**Polymer's band is the whole sheet, near-white to near-black**, which is what moulded plastic actually looks like and was chosen deliberately over a safer light-only band. The cost is stated plainly: Polymer's dark end and Neutronium's are the same brightness, so **brightness alone stops being what tells the ×1 from the ×5**. What tells them apart instead is the surface — Polymer is a dielectric with a visible grain and a broad sheen, Neutronium is full metallic at smoothness 0.05 in a scene with no reflection probe and returns nothing at all. That is a stronger cue at a glance than a value step was, but it is a cue in the *relief*, so the grain here is not decoration: it is the thing carrying the reading. Gloss rides along with the roll rather than being a second die (`darkSmoothness` 0.45 → `lightSmoothness` 0.12), because in real moulded plastic the dark stock is the glossy stock — and because it is the other half of that same distinction.

**`hueJitter` is a fraction of each channel, never an amount added to it.** Added, the same 0.03 is a whisper at 0.93 and a rout at 0.05 — the dark end of the band is only 0.05 wide — and the first dark Polymer block off the additive version came out plainly *brown*. A fraction is the same shift wherever it lands. What it costs is that the darkest blocks barely vary in hue at all, which is right: down there the variety is the grain and the gloss, which is what has to be doing the work anyway.

**One grain density serves four shapes, and the stock cube could not.** `GameManager.GrainTilesPerUnit` (2, tiles of grain per world unit) is divided by `Brick.grainUvPerUnit` — how many UV units that shape's mesh lays across one world unit — so the moulding is the same size on a slab, a half-block, a rounded prism and a sphere, whose meshes lay UVs out four different ways. The stock cube maps 0..1 across *every* face whatever that face measures, so the one tiling that suits a 1.5-wide front is two and a half times too dense across a 0.6-deep side, and the grain came out visibly **combed into stripes** there. No tiling value can fix that — one number was being asked to serve faces of two sizes — so `Brick.prefab` and `HalfBrick.prefab` now wear `BuildWorldUvBoxMesh` meshes where a UV unit is a world unit on all six faces, which is the same thing the rounded prism already did and why that block never had the problem. The geometry is authored at **unit** size with the block's proportions baked into the UVs alone, so the prefabs keep the transform scale their crack overlay is measured in; verified after the swap, the scale is still (1.5, 0.5, 0.6) and the collider untouched. The sphere is left on lat/long UVs and needs no mesh of its own — it has no thin side to comb.

Two things this has not been looked at against yet. **The crack overlay is still one shared sprite set, material-blind**, so a crack on a near-black Polymer block is as untested as the ones on Crystal, Waspaloy and Neutronium already were. And a **coarse grain tiled three times across a slab's face shows its repetition** if looked for; the dial for that is `GrainTilesPerUnit` against the cap radii in `WriteGrainTextures`, which have to move together or the grain's world size moves with it.

### The push can be charged

**DOWN winds up a push, and letting go of it on the bounce hands the charge to the ball.** Held, it fills `Paddle.Charge` over `ChargeTime` (0.8 of a second) and holds there; let go, it becomes a *release* — a charge and the moment it was let go of, standing on the paddle for as long as it could still be worth something. When the ball is caught, `Ball.TakePush` reconciles the two: the charge, times how *early* the release landed, becomes speed on the ball — all of it let go of on the bounce, none of it a full `Ball.PunchWindow` (0.33s) ahead of it, and nothing at all once the ball has gone by (see below). A full charge released on the moment is `PunchTopSpeed` — three times the only speed the ball has ever had. Everything in between is in between, which is the whole of the mechanic.

The window opens **backwards from the bounce and stops there**. A charge let go of while the ball is still coming is a shot aimed early, and how early decides how much of it lands; a charge let go of once the ball has already gone earns nothing at all, however narrowly it missed. Aiming ahead of the ball is the skill.

It opened **both ways** for a while, by the same amount either side, on the reasoning that a player timing a key to a bounce cannot know which side of it they landed on — so refusing the late half would punish half of every honest attempt. What that actually bought was a push applied to a ball that had already left, which is the one thing this mechanic cannot be allowed to look like. The old reasoning is worth keeping in mind rather than acting on: the answer to "the player cannot tell which side they landed on" is a window wide enough to aim inside, not one that pays out behind the bounce.

So the ball still *collects* the push rather than reading it once at the contact, but only for as long as the two clocks could disagree. The catch is timed in `FixedUpdate` and the release in the paddle's own `Update`, in an order nothing fixes, so a release the player made *on* the bounce can be stamped a frame the wrong side of it. `TakePush`'s `grace` is exactly that slop — one physics step plus one frame, written as those two rather than as a number, because it is not a window anything can be played inside — and it exists so that a dead-on release is not the one release the timing refuses. A push can therefore still land a frame into the ball's flight, and nothing downstream can tell: `FixedUpdate` renormalizes the ball to `Speed` every step, so raising it mid-flight is the same act as raising it at the contact.

One release to a catch, now, where the two-sided window had to keep a catch live and let a nearer release raise the result. There is nothing nearer left to wait for once the ball has been caught: every release still to come is later, and later is worth nothing. A late release is not even spent by the ball — the paddle lapses its own, and the charge may yet be the one the player means for the next catch — though the *gauge* has already flown apart, which is the point of a mistimed push: it bursts, and there is nothing to show for it. The ball still takes the **greater** of what it has and what arrives rather than the sum, which is what stops two pushes in a rally compounding into a ball nothing can be done about: `PunchTopSpeed` is meant to be the fastest the ball is ever seen, so a push onto a ball still carrying one is a top-up and not a stack.

The extra speed **bleeds** rather than counting down (`PunchDecay` 0.7 a second, half of what is left every second), and the loud part of it is over quickly on purpose: a pushed ball is a *shot*, and one that stayed fast for a whole rally would make the paddle's own speed the thing that felt wrong. Measured off a 0.80 push: 0.55, 0.39, 0.27 over the first second and a half, and down to 0.03 by five seconds. `PunchFloor` puts it back to exactly the ball's own speed at the bottom, because an exponential bleed never quite arrives and a ball permanently a hair fast would be a lie told in every number downstream of `speed`. A serve owes nothing to it either: `Launch` and `AttachTo` both clear it.

It is the ball's `speed` that everything is read through, never a second number — `Ball.Speed` is `speed × (1 + punch)`, and the angle floor, the pinned-ball test, the stall escape and the paddle's own arcade bounce all get the boosted speed without knowing there is one. Which is also why a pushed ball caught again bounces off at the speed it arrived with: measured, a catch at 7.21 on a ball still carrying 0.24 of a push.

**A push is weight as well as speed, and a broken block is not a surface.** `Ball.Damage` is the authored 1 times `SpeedMultiple` — how many of its own speeds the ball is travelling at — so a ball at 2.5 does 2.5 and takes a full-slot slab (hardness 2) out in a single hit, where the ball at its own speed still does the 1 it always did. A charge is therefore worth aiming at a *wall* and not only at a gap.

Breaking a block costs the ball the block's hardness out of its speed, in the same multiples both are counted in, floored at 1 — nothing may leave the ball slower than the ball. What is left over is the whole of the rule:

- **Still over its own speed** and the ball carries straight on, through the hole it just made and into whatever stands behind it. One shot can take a row.
- **Back down to its own speed** and it comes off the block it broke like any other bounce. The push is spent, and the last thing it buys is the block. A ball at 2.5 through a hardness-2 slab lands exactly here: 0.5 is under 1, so it is floored to 1 and the ball bounces.

Carrying on means **undoing a bounce the engine has already applied**, since the contact is resolved before any of this runs. `Ball.wayIn` is the heading the ball took into the physics step — written on every path out of `FixedUpdate`, which runs before the step it belongs to — and a punch-through puts the ball back on it. Its speed is not restored with it: `FixedUpdate` renormalizes to `Speed` every step and `Speed` has just come down, so what carries on is the line and not the pace. `flatBounces` is reset too, because a block gone through is progress by any reading and the stalled-rally count is there for a ball being batted between two faces.

**Which side owns the hit had to change for this.** `Brick.TakeDamage` is public now and answers whether the block was destroyed, and the *ball* calls it out of its own collision handler, where the block used to read `ball.Damage` out of one of its own. The answer decides whether the ball comes off or carries on, and the two handlers for one contact run in an order nothing fixes — so the ball cannot wait to be told by a block whose turn may already have passed. A force field answers `false`: it was not destroyed, so the ball bounces off it and keeps every bit of the push, since unbreakable that cost the ball its charge would be a much smaller word. A block's accumulated `damage` is a float now for the same reason the hit is.

**Nothing about it argues with anything else the paddle does.** DOWN is a key of its own — SPACE is the thrust and the serve, the arrows steer — so a charge is wound on the run, under boost, on a paddle jammed against the frame, whichever. The menu's paddle carries the same component, so the menu's ball can be shot across its screen too.

**The charge is visible as a gauge under the paddle** (`PowerWave`): three waves standing in the empty strip below it, clear of the paddle's underside, the first the widest — noticeably narrower than the paddle itself — and each one after it smaller, so what they make is a pyramid stood on its point — broad where it meets the paddle and tapering away below. The first fills, and only once it is at its full width does the second begin to appear. So **the number standing full is what the charge is** — one wave is a third of it, two is two thirds, three is all of it and a 3× ball — and three is a count nobody has to actually count.

The waves get **smaller as they go and brighter as they go**, which is one fact told twice: the charge is being driven to a point, so the last wave is the smallest and the fiercest, and how far down the taper the bright end has reached is the same reading as how many waves are full. It also puts the hottest colour where there is most room for it, out in the dark strip below rather than up against the pale underside of the paddle. The ramp is deep blue → blue → light cornflower, eased (`RampEase`) so the middle sits nearer the first than the last, since spaced evenly the middle and the last were close enough to be taken for each other.

Each wave is a **stadium, not a bar** — a box with a round cap at each end, which is why a wave is three pieces rather than one. A rounded-rectangle mesh, the kind the paddle and the rounded brick are cut from, cannot serve: a wave's width is not a fixed number, it grows as the wave fills, and scaling one mesh across that range stretches the corner radius with it, so every wave would be rounded by a different amount and each one differently from moment to moment. Caps scaled *uniformly* by the wave's own thickness are round by construction whatever the box between them is doing, and the box is shortened by their diameter so the wave's whole width is still its width. A wave narrower than it is thick is all cap — the two meet and it reads as a dot growing into a bar, which is the right picture for one that has only just appeared.

The gap between waves is measured **edge to edge, not centre to centre** (`Gap`, with the offsets worked out once into `Drops`). The three are of three different thicknesses, so evenly-spaced centres leave more daylight between the thin ones at the bottom than between the thick ones at the top, and the stack read as spreading out as it went down. The table is precomputed rather than accumulated while drawing because a wave that is not up yet must not shift the ones below it.

They are cold where the exhaust is hot, and that is deliberate for the same reason the taper points away from the paddle: the two mechanics live on the same paddle, so a blue pyramid under it can never be mistaken for an orange flame streaming behind it. All three full takes the lot a little towards white and throbs it noticeably harder (`ReadyGlow`, `ReadyPulse`), since a charge past full gains nothing and every frame spent holding it is a frame the ball is closer. Letting go plays the gauge *apart*: whatever was standing widens out past its full width, thins away and is gone over `SpentTime`. That is the picture of a charge spent whole, and it plays whether or not there was a ball to take it — a release that missed its window is the mistake the player most needs to see they made.

Two versions of this were wrong before it, and both were wrong in the same direction — pretty, and mute:

- **A flowing ladder of waves came first.** Bars rose out of the strip into the paddle's underside on a cadence that quickened as the charge filled. It looked better than this does and it *read* far worse: a cadence and a colour say "more" without ever saying how much, and how much is the only thing the player needs off this. A charge is a number the player is committing to a tenth of a second in advance, so the picture of it has to be countable.
- **A full wave went to near-white**, twice — first for every full wave, then just for the last one. Near-white sitting directly under a light grey paddle read as a highlight on the paddle rather than as a wave that had filled, and none of the count is legible if the eye cannot tell the top of the gauge from the bottom of the paddle. The deeper reason it cannot work: these are Lit surfaces under one directional light, so nothing on screen can be brighter than white, and a near-white bar therefore reads as *unlit paddle grey* rather than as the fiercest thing in the picture. The ramp stays inside the blue family the whole way — a saturated cyan is lower in measured luminance and hotter to look at, which is the only kind of bright available here. "Ready" is carried mostly by the throb for the same reason.
- **The throb swung both ways.** At the depth a full gauge pulses at, that could put a lower wave in a trough while the one above it was at a peak — standing the gauge's own ordering on its head a fraction of a second at a time. It only brightens now, and by less than the step between one wave's colour and the next: whatever else it does, the last wave has to stay the brightest.
- **The stack started too close to the paddle** (`Clearance`, a third of a paddle height, now 0.7 of one). Tucked up under it the first wave was crowded by the paddle's own bottom edge and the shadow beneath it, and a wave with its top cropped *looks narrower than it is* — so the widest wave read as the smallest and the taper appeared to run the other way. The whole shape depends on the first wave being seen whole.
- **The first wave matched the paddle's width** (`Widths`, 0.95 of it, now 0.75). At that width it read as the paddle having a coloured underside rather than as a wave standing below one: the gauge needs daylight at both ends to be a thing of its own.
- **The colours were written straight into `_BaseColor`.** The project renders in **linear colour space** (`m_ActiveColorSpace: 1`), so a colour handed to a property block from script is taken as already-linear and comes out considerably paler and less saturated than the number reads — an authored 0.45 green landed around 0.70 on screen, which is how a strong blue ramp turned up looking like a washed-out cyan one. The material Inspector does that conversion for a colour picked by hand and a property block does not. The ramp is now authored and blended in sRGB, where "halfway between these two blues" means what it looks like, and converted once with `.linear` on the way to the shader. Worth knowing before tuning any of the other cosmetics' colours, which were all eyeballed in the wrong space.
- **The ramp left the blue family at both ends.** Taking the green out of it to deepen the low end made an indigo that went purple against the room's cold murk; taking the green *up* to brighten the high end made a teal. Both stop being "the one bright blue thing on the screen", which is the whole job. The low end keeps its green and the hot end has less green than an even light cyan would — a light cornflower, plainly blue at any brightness.

**A pushed ball burns, and the fire is the push you can still spend.** Past `Ball.WakeSpeed` — half again the one speed the ball has ever had — it trails a blue jet flame, and how long, thick and bright that flame is says how much of the push is left. It is the same `JetTrail` the paddle's rocket is made of, asked for in a different colour (`JetTrail.Plasma` against `JetTrail.Rocket`): the same swept-path plume off the trailing edge, the same embers on a cadence, the same length-chosen-and-lifetime-derived rule, every size a fraction of the ball's own drawn diameter so the menu's smaller ball trails a smaller flame. What differs is only the colour it burns, which is the point — the player learns one shape and reads the colour.

That closes the loop the gauge opens. The charge is wound up in blue under the paddle and comes back out as blue behind the ball: the gauge is the push going in and the wake is the push coming out, and the paddle's own orange exhaust can never be read as either. It also gives the bleed a reading it never had — `PunchDecay` is a fact only the rally could feel, and the flame thinning out over the two seconds after the catch is that fact on screen.

**Half again is the threshold, and it is read off `Speed` rather than off `punch`**, so it is written in the units a player would describe it in and stays true if the ball's own speed is ever retuned. A fifth of a push is a fifth: a trail on one would be a trail behind almost every catch, which is a light that is merely on rather than a reading. It also sits comfortably clear of `PunchFloor`, so the last flicker of a bleeding push cannot flash a frame of flame on its way out. Measured at the top of the range: strength 1.00 at a speed of 17.42 on the menu's ball (3× its 5.81), the flame white-cored at the ball and deep blue at its tail; at 1.7× the same wake is slimmer and stays blue the whole way, which is the ramp doing its job at the quiet end.

**An exhaust is measured in two units, and they are two because the ball wants a short wide jet.** `JetTrail` takes a `bore` — the nozzle: how wide the flame is across and how big its sparks are — and a `reach`, the unit its ribbon's *length* is counted in. The paddle hands its own height in for both, which is where the single number came from originally: a rocket bolted to the back of a slab is as wide as the slab. The ball hands in two fractions of its own diameter (`WakeBore` 0.6, `WakeReach` a third), and those are its size dials rather than anything in `JetTrail`, whose proportions the paddle's rocket shares.

One unit could only offer both or neither, and both readings were wrong in turn. At the ball's full diameter the wake was a beam the ball rode on the end of — a flame as wide as its nozzle is not a flame — and a ribbon measured in the same unit crossed a good part of the field. A third of the diameter for both fixed the length and left a flame too thin to be one. Wide and short is two numbers.

**A bounce breaks the wake rather than bending it.** Every contact drops `burning`, in the same place and for the same reason the contact scrubs the spin — everything after it in `OnCollisionEnter2D` can return early, and every contact there is turns the ball. A bounce is a corner in the flight and a flame does not go round one: without this the frame after a bounce swept one plume from the nozzle's place *before* it to its place after, a single piece lying across the corner, and the whole trail read as hinged rather than as trailing. Dropping the flag is the whole of the fix, because the pieces already in the air are unparented and carry the heading they were born with — so the old ribbon goes on thinning away along the line it was laid on while a new one rises out of the ball on the new heading. Dissolve and rise, not a turn.

Two things fall out of the ball not being the paddle. **Behind it is not an axis**: the paddle's exhaust runs along ±X, where the ball flies at whatever angle it was last given and curves while it does (`CurveRate`), so each frame's plume is laid along the heading of that moment and the ribbon bends with the flight for nothing. And **the sweep is remembered rather than derived** — the nozzle's place last frame is kept, where the paddle works its own out from the frame's move — with `burning` saying whether there is a previous frame worth sweeping from at all: without it the plume that opens a wake would be laid from wherever the ball happened to last be fast, which on a fresh push is halfway across the field. A serve clears both along with everything else `AttachTo` clears, since a served ball is at its own speed and has nothing to burn.

**The brightness is a multiply, not a lerp towards white** (`JetTrail.GlowTop`). The palette is scaled by up to 2.4 and the project renders in HDR, so the core of a hard-burning ribbon pushes past white with blue still around it; lerping to white instead bleached the flame out at exactly the moment it is meant to be most itself. Both palettes are written in the space the shader takes them in — already-linear, not sRGB — because they have to be tuned against each other, which is why the blue reads improbably dark written down: 0.03 of green is a good 0.19 on screen (the trap `PowerWave`'s ramp fell into, from the other side).

The gauge is made once, on the paddle's first charge, and is **not parented to the paddle** — the paddle's transform is scaled on the menu screen and everything the gauge is measured by is a world size, so the paddle drives its position instead and switches it off in `OnDisable`, since the menu's paddle is switched off wholesale when the menu leaves and a gauge left standing would be a charge glowing under a paddle that is not there. Everything it is measured by comes off the paddle's own drawn body, so the menu's scaled-down paddle charges with a scaled-down gauge. A gentle brightening runs down the stack a wave at a time (`PulseRate`, `PulsePhase`) — all that is left of the waves actually being waves, and enough: a gauge sitting perfectly still would read as a UI widget bolted under the paddle rather than as something being gathered.

## The main menu screen

The menu is **a room of its own**, a screen's width (`MainMenuPanel.ScreenSpacing`) to the left of the playfield in world X — it does not stand in front of the playfield any more. That is what gives START something to travel across: `GameManager.TravelToPlayfield` builds the round first and then slides the camera right from the menu's room to the playfield's over `TravelDuration` (0.6 s), switching the menu off and raising the HUD on arrival, in the same frame the camera lands so the menu's backdrop is never seen over the round. Nothing but the camera moves. `traveling` holds off every key on the way (the ball is already sitting on the paddle), and `MenuBackdropWidth` (24) is enough for the menu's backdrop to fill its own room's frame at any sane aspect ratio. It may overhang the playfield's room freely — the menu is switched off in the same frame the view arrives there, so the only time this backdrop is seen from outside its own room is on the journey between the two, which is exactly where it should be. Coming *back* — ESC, or a round that has ended — is a cut rather than a journey: `ShowMenu` puts the camera over the menu in the same frame the opaque menu comes up, so there is nothing to see. A lost round is cut back the same way, onto the menu's game over board rather than its title board (see "The board a lost round ends on"). Because the rooms are apart in X, `Paddle` clamps to `xLimit` either side of **where it was authored** (`homeX`, taken in `Awake`) rather than either side of x 0, which would drag the menu's paddle out of its own room.

The menu is not a UI panel at all — it is a small playable scene, and there is no UGUI in it. It is also **three screens side by side**, spaced `MainMenuPanel.ScreenSpacing` (20 units) apart and in this order in X: the hall of fame, the board a lost round ends on, the title board. All three hang off `MenuSlider`, and picking any of them travels there rather than cutting to another view. **Every arrow scrolls the way it points**, which is what settles that order: the left-pointing HALL OF FAME arrow travels left to reach the hall, so the hall is on the left, and the right-pointing arrow on the hall travels right to come back — as the right-pointing START does to leave the menu for a round. The game over board has one of each, leading to the hall and to the menu, which is what puts it *between* those two and leaves the title board's own HALL OF FAME arrow travelling two screens past it. The champion arrows follow the same rule (see below). The `MenuScreen` root (authored inactive, carrying `MainMenuPanel` itself) holds:

- `MenuBackdrop` — the far side of the fog (see "A screen change happens in depth" below) rather than a wall standing behind the screens: an opaque matte box at `MenuBackdropZ` (−2.95), `MenuBackdropWidth` (28) across, covering the menu room's frame on any sane aspect ratio, wearing the fog's own colour (`ScreenChange.FogColor`). Outside the slider, so it stays put while the screens travel across it. Its front face is `ScreenChange.FogWall` (0.55) behind the playing plane, which is deliberately close: this gap is the length of every shadow the menu throws. It stood a unit and a half back for a while, to leave an arriving screen room to fly in behind the plane and still be seen, and every letter on the menu trailed a shadow for it.
- `MenuFogFar` and `MenuFogNear` — the two drifting banks of haze that make the fog fog rather than a wall (`MenuFog`), hanging in front of that backdrop at 0.42 and 0.3 behind the playing plane. Outside the slider with it, since the fog is the room's weather and not something a screen carries in with it.
- `MenuSlider/MenuBoard` — `MenuTitle`, the word ARKANOID, tilted 8° so its tops and sides catch the light and it throws a shadow onto the backdrop. It is **eight children, one brick per letter**, each with a `MenuTitleBlock` and a `BoxCollider2D` covering the glyph's whole 5 × 7 box (holes included — a collider tracing the strokes would let the ball rattle around inside an O). A hit shatters the letter into `Debris` and switches it off; `MainMenuPanel` switches them all back on when the menu opens and whenever this board is the one travelling into the frame, so breaking the word is a toy rather than a permanent edit. Beside it stand the two options: **START is a right-pointing arrow banner to the right of the word, HALL OF FAME a left-pointing one to its left**, at ±`MenuArrowX` (6.45), which leaves about 0.6 of a unit both between an arrow and the word and between an arrow and the frame's edge.
- `MenuSlider/MenuOver` — the board a lost round ends on: GAME OVER over the score, between the other two (see below).
- `MenuSlider/MenuHall` — the hall of fame (see below).
- `MenuPlay` — the part the player drives: a `MenuPaddle` reusing the playfield paddle's mesh, collider and `Paddle` script, and a `MenuBall` instance of the ball prefab at the round's own rate scaled to the menu's nearer plane (`MenuBallSpeed` — see "The playfield is the frame"). Outside the slider too, so the paddle and ball stay where they are while the world behind them moves.

Everything hittable sits on the `MenuPlaneZ` −3.7 plane.

**Picking an option means hitting its arrow with the ball**, and the arrow breaks under it: the hit shatters it into `Debris` in its own colour and switches it off, and `MainMenuPanel` holds the choice back for `ShatterPause` (0.9 s) before acting on it, so the pieces visibly fall before the screen changes rather than the hit cutting away instantly. `OnOptionHit` refuses every hit after the first, which is what stops the still-live ball from picking a second option during the pause, and refuses an arrow belonging to the screen that isn't up, since both screens are in the scene at once and only the slide separates them. **A refused arrow doesn't shatter** — it bounces the ball like the frame does, and `OnOptionHit`'s `bool` answer is what keeps it standing. That isn't cosmetic: an arrow is a screen's only way out, and the only mending it gets is on the way *in* to its board (below), so an arrow broken on the board already in the frame would stay broken. Shattering refused hits was tried, on the theory that an arrow merely bouncing the ball reads as an impact the game failed to notice, and it stranded the player: knock out HALL OF FAME with a stray bounce during a change and the title screen has no way to reach the hall at all. The lettering is the toy; the arrows are the controls. Every arrow is a banner — a body drawn out to a point at one end, corners rounded — with its label (START, HALL OF / FAME, PREV, MENU) **set into its front face**: the banner's own mesh carries a pocket the shape of the word (one mesh per option — `MenuArrowStart`, `MenuArrowHall`, `MenuArrowPrev`, `MenuArrowBack` — rather than one per direction), and small white blocks (`MenuInlay*`, the same block-font geometry the title is built from, in the pale `MenuLabel` material) sit down in it as an `ArrowInlay` child. The banner is sized for the gaps around it first and the lettering follows, which is why `MenuArrowLabelCell` is as small as it is. The label is centred on the *body* rather than the whole shape, so the point stays empty and the letters don't run out past the slants.

**The blocks are what is read; the pocket is only what says they are sunk.** A groove on its own was tried — the letters cut out of the face and nothing put back — and it read as a smudge: the floor of a groove faces the camera exactly as the face around it does and is lit exactly the same, so at this size there was nothing to see but two hairlines of chamfer. What the pocket is for is the shadow line around blocks that are below the surface rather than on it.

**A pocket wall has to lean**, for the same reason. The menu is looked at head-on, so a wall standing straight down has no width on screen at all. Every wall is therefore a 45° chamfer, and `MenuArrowPocketSubdivisions` (3) sets it: the pocket is the lettering at three times its resolution grown by one sub-cell all round, so its mouth flares a third of a cell past the blocks and its floor lies a third of a cell down. A third rather than a half because at a half the pockets of two strokes one cell apart would meet. The light — head-on, pitched down by `LightPitch` — does the rest: the wall above a stroke faces away from it and goes dark, the wall below faces into it and comes up brighter than the face around it. The blocks stand `MenuArrowInlayLift` off the pocket's floor, which is nothing to see and everything to the depth buffer — floor and block are the same shape, so without it they would fight for the same pixels.

The `PolygonCollider2D` is the arrow's own rounded outline, so a hit anywhere reflects off the shape the player can see, corners included. Green means "on you go" (START, and the way back from the hall), blue means "the other way" (HALL OF FAME, and the record before the one on the plaque). Debris fragments are unparented root objects that outlive their spawner, so `Hide` calls `Debris.ClearAll()` — otherwise the menu's rubble would go on raining over the first seconds of the round.

Only `StartGame` is ever raised to `GameManager` as `OptionChosen`; the hall of fame and the way back are the menu's own business, carried out by `CarryOut` — slide, or advance the champion — after which the shattered arrow is put back and nothing else is (the board being travelled to was mended before it set off; whatever the ball broke on the way stays broken — see below).

### A screen change happens in depth

**A screen change is two movements, not one, and they are not even in the same direction** (`ScreenChange`, shared by the menu's two boards and by the hall's champions). The screen being left flies out across the frame in the playing plane, solid the whole way. The screen arriving never crosses the frame at all: it is already standing where it belongs, down in **the fog** behind the playing plane, and it **rises out of the fog** into place. It used to be one slide of `MenuSlider` carrying both screens past each other in the same plane; the slider is only the layout now, and each screen is moved by a `ScreenPiece` of its own, which remembers where its transforms rest so the motion can be written as an offset and undone by putting the offset back. The one frame where the slider takes over the departed screen's offset moves nothing on screen: the slider gains exactly what that screen gives up — and it is the frame that carries the arriving screen into the middle of the picture, which is why that screen is put down into the fog (`ScreenChange.Stage`) in the same frame.

**The fog is what the space between the playing plane and the backdrop is**, rather than a gap with a wall at the end of it. It is a hand's breadth deep — `FogWall` (0.55), which is where the backdrop's front face stands — because every shadow on the menu is thrown onto that backdrop, and a shadow's drop is its object's gap from it times the tangent of `LightPitch`. Keeping the wall close keeps the shadows short, which is the whole point of the fog: what gives an arriving screen somewhere to be is not distance but the murk. `ScreenPiece.SetFog` mixes the fog's colour (`FogColor`, which is also the colour the backdrop's material wears) into whatever a screen's renderers wear, per instance through a `MaterialPropertyBlock`, so one screen can be in the fog while the other is not; the deeper the screen, the more of it. **The sheen goes with the colour**, and it has to: the backdrop is matte, so a screen wearing the fog's colour and still catching a highlight is given away by the one thing the colour cannot cover — a fully fogged option arrow stood out from the wall by thirty levels of grey on nothing but its own gloss. A screen waits at `FogDepth` (0.9), which is *behind* the backdrop — out of sight entirely, since 0.9 clears the wall by more than the half-depth of the thickest thing a screen carries — and by the time it has risen far enough to clear the wall's face it is wearing the fog's own colour outright, so it emerges from the murk rather than appearing in front of it. It also casts no shadow until it is more than half out (`ScreenPiece.ShadowFog`): a shadow does not fade with its caster, and a crisp one beside a dim shape would draw the eye straight to the thing meant to be barely there.

**The fog moves**, which is what stops it being a wall painted to look like fog. Two banks of haze (`MenuFog`) hang inside it — `MenuFogFarDepth` (0.42) and `MenuFogNearDepth` (0.3) behind the playing plane, both clear of the thickest thing standing on it — as wide as the backdrop, so their own edges are never in frame. Each is a transparent unlit sheet wearing one tileable cloud (`Assets/Textures/Fog.png`, generated: three octaves of wrapping value noise, white with the whole shape in the alpha), and each walks the texture's UVs across itself at its own rate, in its own direction, at its own scale. Nothing is simulated: the fluidity is the interference between the two, and a hundredth of a tile a second is slow enough that the fog is never caught moving and only ever noticed by having moved. The haze is **lighter and bluer than the fog's own colour** — a first attempt used the fog colour itself and changed nothing at all when it drifted, since haze the same darkness as the murk behind it is invisible. It hangs in front of a sinking screen as well as in front of the wall, so a screen rises *through* the weather rather than behind it; because both are veiled by the same drift, a fully fogged screen stays as lost against the wall as it was without any.

**A screen in the fog is not in the game.** Its colliders are off for the whole arrival, so the ball passes over it while it rises — which is also what stops the ball breaking a board that is rising into it. This is not the "transparent ball" that was tried and taken out (see below): the ball stays solid to everything, and it is the arriving screen, which is not in the playing plane yet, that is not there to be hit. The screen being left is solid throughout, so the rally carries on against it exactly as it did.

**A screen rising into a ball lifts it out of the plane** rather than being broken by it or shouldering it aside. `ScreenChange.Carry` finds the face rising under the ball and hands its depth to `Ball.PushInFrontOf`; the ball rides its own radius clear of that face, towards the camera, and drops back at `PlaneReturn` once nothing is holding it up. 2D physics ignores Z, so none of this touches the rally — the ball keeps its heading and its speed throughout, and only its depth (and its shadow, which separates as it comes forward) says it has been lifted. The screen goes solid again only once the ball is back in the plane and clear of it, so it never becomes solid inside the ball it is holding up; `MaxRide` (2 s) is the backstop for a ball that is not travelling, which is a ball waiting to be served on the paddle. The lift needs no timing of its own: a face still down in the fog is further from the ball than the ball's own radius, so `PushInFrontOf` asks for nothing until the screen has actually reached it.

The one thing the plaque still pays for is the shadow that is left. The hall of fame is the only place two lines of lettering stand above each other, and the name's shadow drops a fifth of a unit onto the score's line, so the two stand 1.75 apart rather than the 1.5 they were authored at (see `HallOfFame.LineShadowGap`). They were 2.25 apart while the backdrop stood well back and that shadow was a whole unit long.

**The rally carries on across a screen change**: the ball is not caught and re-served when the menu moves between its screens, because the paddle and the ball are the one thing on the menu that isn't a screen, and stopping them for one made the menu feel like a series of pages rather than one game being played. The ball stays solid throughout, and hitting whatever is passing in front of it is part of that: it was tried the other way — the ball made "transparent" to both boards from the moment a choice was made until its next bounce, so nothing could be knocked apart mid-change — and it played worse than leaving the ball alone, whether the transparency ended at the next bounce or was held to the end of the change. Do not reach for it again without a clearer reason than tidiness. `busy` no longer holds off the rest of `Update` either: the ball still has to be caught when it falls out of the frame and served again when it does, and all `busy` means now is that a second option can't be picked (`OnOptionHit`).

There is no keyboard or mouse path to the options. The ball waits on the paddle until SPACE, exactly like a round — `GameManager.OnGUI` draws the same "press SPACE to launch" prompt (lower on screen for the menu, where mid-screen is where the options are). The paddle rests under the middle, between the two arrows, so a ball launched straight up picks nothing and the player has to aim.

**The menu's field is the frame itself**, as a round's is. `GameManager.playfieldObjects` (the `Playfield` root and the playfield `Paddle`) is switched off for the whole `Menu` state and back on in `NewGame`: 2D physics ignores Z, so the playfield's colliders would otherwise fence in the menu's ball — the rooms are apart in X, but a ball driven out of the menu's room would still find them — and both paddles would answer the arrow keys at once. In their place the menu builds three walls of its own (`Border`, shared with the playfield) and lays them along the left, right and top edges of what the camera actually sees, inner faces exactly on the frame, so the ball **ricochets off the edge of the screen**. They are built at runtime by `MainMenuPanel.FitToFrame` rather than authored, for the same reason the option arrows are pulled in there: the frame is only known once there is a window, and it changes when the window does. They carry no renderer — the border is the screen's edge, not an object — so what says the ball hit something is the `Ricochet` burst spawned at the point of contact. The frame is measured as extents and then centred on the *room* rather than on the camera, since the view travels out of this room to the playfield with the menu still up; the paddle's travel comes off the same measurement. The bottom is left open: a ball that falls past the paddle materialises back on it, and `OutOfFrame` still tests all four sides as the way home for a ball a resized frame left on the wrong side of a wall. `Paddle.cs` keeps its own Z rather than zeroing it, so the menu's paddle stays on the menu plane.

### The hall of fame

`MenuHall` shows **one champion at a time**: their name over their score, in the same block letters as the title, with a left arrow to the record before it and a right arrow back to the menu — the arrow the player came in on, mirrored. The names come from the record book, so both lines are built at runtime by `HallOfFame` rather than authored; the meshes are its own and are destroyed as it moves on. The list is `RecordBook.Load()` sorted by score, best first, capped at `MaxChampions` (10), and it is **walked down rather than cycled**: the plaque opens on the standing record and the left arrow steps back through the records that one beat, with no wrap and no way back up. The walk ends on the oldest record kept, where the arrow is simply not there any more — the cycle it replaced invited the player to ride the same ten names round for ever. The champion does not change on the spot: `HallOfFame.Advance` is a coroutine that makes **the same change the menu's two boards make** (see "A screen change happens in depth") — the champion standing there flies out across the frame the way the left-pointing arrow that was hit points, and the next one rises out of the fog behind it. Each champion's symbols hang off a group of their own under each line, one `ScreenPiece` for the pair, which is what lets the one leaving and the one arriving be in two different places, and at two different depths, at the same time; the lines themselves never move. The arriving champion is put down into the fog in the frame they are built, since `Render` builds them where the plaque rests and the champion they replace is still standing there. **The arrow that was hit rises with them**: it shattered to make the choice, and it belongs to the champion it fetched, so it is switched back on down in the fog and comes up with the name and the score. The piece reads the arrow's rest position off the arrow itself (that is `MainMenuPanel`'s fit to the frame, so it can't be written down here) and puts it back there. `MainMenuPanel.RestoreOption` still runs afterwards and finds it already standing. The symbols' UV origins are their place in the word, so the masonry reads the same wherever the plaque is while it is built. Only real records are shown; an empty book reads **EMPTY** on the name's line with the score's line left bare (a sentence there would read as a champion called NO RECORDS YET, which is what it used to say), and the "previous record" arrow is hidden whenever there is nothing older to walk back to — on an empty book, and on the last champion, where it does not even travel in with the plaque it fetched. Both lines share one cell size, taken from the longer of the two against the `MaxWidth` (9) units the plaque has and capped at `CellCap`. **The cap is the gap between the two lines, not a number written down**: a glyph is `GlyphHeight` cells tall and is drawn centred on its line, so the pair only fits while a glyph plus `LineShadowGap` covers the distance between them, and `CellCap` measures that distance off the authored transforms rather than assuming it. A short name is held back by the cap rather than by the width — "AGX" over "300" has width to spare, and the old fixed `MaxCell` of 0.25 blew it up to 1.75 units a line across the 1.5 the lines then stood apart, so the name's feet stood in the top of the score. `LineShadowGap` (0.35) is what has to stay clear between them, and it is **the length of the name's shadow** rather than a cell of letter-spacing: the plaque stands `ScreenChange.FogWall` clear of the backdrop it throws that shadow onto, and a shadow drops that gap times the tangent of `LightPitch`, which comes to a fifth of a unit, plus a little air. That is why the lines stand 1.75 apart (`HallNameY` 2.75, `HallScoreY` 1) rather than the 1.5 they were authored at, at which any cell big enough to read threw the name's shadow across the score. They were 2.25 apart while the backdrop stood well back and the shadow was a whole unit long; the fog brought the wall back in and the lines with it. At 1.75 the cap works out at exactly the title's own `MenuTitleCell` (0.2), which is right: the title should stay the largest lettering on the menu. Move a line in the scene and the lettering resizes to match.

**Every symbol of both lines is its own hittable block**, exactly like a letter of the title: one `GameObject` per glyph under the line, carrying its own mesh, a `BoxCollider2D` over the glyph's whole 5 × 7 box and the same `MenuTitleBlock` the title's letters use, so a name and a score can be knocked apart while they are read. That building is `BlockLine`, shared with the game over board's score line, since both are lettering nobody could have authored. A glyph with nothing solid in it (a space) is skipped — nothing to hit and nothing to show. The line object itself holds no mesh any more; it is the anchor, and its `MeshRenderer` is where the symbols take their material from. The symbols are tracked in a list rather than counted off the line's children, because `Destroy` only takes effect at the end of the frame and the children would still be last champion's. Walking back to the record before rebuilds the plaque outright, and a champion arrived at with half their name missing is a record that can't be read.

**A board is mended on the way in, and never once it is in the frame** (`MainMenuPanel.RestoreBoard`, lettering and the arrows standing beside it together). The rule falls out of the ball playing on through a change: the boards move with the ball still live, so the player watches the hits it lands on them, and putting those letters back the instant the movement stops reads as the game undoing hits it had already shown — which is exactly how it looked when `CarryOut` ended by mending both boards wholesale. So what the ball breaks *during* a change stays broken, and the board carries that damage until it is next the board arriving, which is the one moment nobody can see it being mended. The only thing put back at the end of a change is the arrow that carried it out (`RestoreOption`) — it shattered to make the choice, and without it the board has one fewer way out than it was built with. Reopening the menu still mends everything (`Show`), and the title is no longer the exception that stayed broken until it did.

The letter meshes come from `BlockText`, which lives in the **runtime** assembly precisely because of this screen — a champion's name is only known while the game is running, so the editor setup script and the hall of fame have to share one builder. It holds the 5 × 7 block font (the whole alphabet and the digits, since a name is whatever the player typed, with anything else falling back to `?`) and `BuildMesh`, which makes one box per horizontal run of solid cells in a glyph row rather than one per cell. UVs are taken from each corner's position plus a `uvOrigin` giving the piece's place in the word (× `MenuTitleUvScale` 2), which is what lets the title be eight independently-built letters whose `BrickWall.png` masonry still runs continuously across the joins. The 47-cell word spans 9.4 units at `MenuTitleCell` 0.2, with the arrows filling the rest of the frame out to its edges — the layout is the whole width of a 16:9 screen rather than the 4:3 box that used to hold it in and kept the word small. A narrower screen is handled at runtime instead of on paper: `MainMenuPanel.FitOptionsToFrame` pulls each arrow in from the frame edge it would otherwise hang over, keeping the same gap the 16:9 layout leaves, re-running whenever the window changes size, so the 16:9 layout is the widest the arrows are ever placed rather than the only one that works. `ArrowOutline` gives the arrow banner's shape — corners rounded by `RoundedOutline`, which replaces each corner of a counter-clockwise polygon with an arc tangent to both its edges and trims the radius where an edge is too short (the point is a sharp angle that would otherwise swallow half the shape) — and `BuildArrowMesh` extrudes exactly that, working out each face's winding from the direction it should look rather than hand-winding the two mirrored arrows separately. `LinesCells` stacks a label too long for one line, which is how HALL OF FAME fits across a banner.

Handed a `BlockText.Engraving`, `BuildArrowMesh` sinks that pocket into the front face instead of drawing it whole, and it does so **without a general triangulator and without a word about corners**. `Pocket` builds the grid: the lettering at N times its resolution, grown by one sub-cell all round. The face outside that block is the outline clipped to the four regions around it — the outline is convex, so each region is convex too and each still fans from its own centre (`Clip` is one half-plane of Sutherland–Hodgman). Inside the block the face is **a height field on the sub-cell grid**: a corner of the grid lies on the pocket's floor where all four cells meeting it are pocket, and up at the face otherwise, and every cell is drawn as one quad through its four corners' depths. Neighbouring cells then share those depths by construction, so the chamfer comes out mitred at every outside corner of a stroke and dimpled at every inside one with nothing written about either, and the surface cannot have a hole in it. Flat runs are merged so most of the block is a handful of quads rather than one per cell.

That the floor comes out as *exactly* the lettering is the same fact stated twice: dropping only the corners whose four cells are all pocket is an erosion by one sub-cell, and eroding a dilation by the same step gives the original back — as long as no gap in the lettering is thinner than two sub-cells, which at any sensible subdivision no block glyph has. That is what lets the white blocks be built from the same cells at the same size and still fit the floor they cover. The first attempt fitted floors and walls together by hand instead, with an explicit patch for inside corners; every awkward shape in the font was its own case, and the height field replaced all of it.

### The board a lost round ends on

**A round that runs out of lives ends on a screen of the menu rather than on an overlay over the round.** `MenuOver` carries `GameOverBoard`: the words GAME OVER in the title's own block letters and masonry, the score the round finished with spelled out under them, a left arrow to the hall of fame and a right arrow to the menu. `GameManager.ShowEndScreen` hands a lost round straight to `ShowMenu(score)`, which is the same cut back to the menu's room that ESC makes — the state becomes `Menu`, the menu opens on this board instead of the title board (`MainMenuPanel.ShowGameOver`), and from there the menu drives itself exactly as it always does. There is no end-screen state to sit in and no key to press to leave one: **the ball is served on the paddle and the player aims at an arrow**, which is the same thing every other choice on this menu is. `State.GameOver` survives only as an `endState`, the thing `EndRound` remembers while name entry happens; nothing is ever in it.

That the two arrows lead to the hall of fame and to the menu is what fixes the board's place in the slider — between the two, because every arrow scrolls the way it points — and it is why the hall of fame stands **two** screens from the title board now rather than one. A change is measured off where the boards were authored (`MainMenuPanel.HomeOf`) rather than off `ScreenSpacing`, so a two-screen change works out for itself; `ScreenChange.FlyOut` takes its *duration* from the distance too, at the rate a screen's width in `FlyOutDuration` sets, since a board crossing the frame twice as fast to cover twice the ground reads as a different change rather than as a longer one.

Both HALL OF FAME and MENU now stand on two boards each, and they lead to the same place from either — which is why **which board an arrow stands on, rather than which choice it carries, is what says whether it can be picked** (`MainMenuPanel.BoardOf`, walking up to the slider's own child). `MenuOption` hands the panel the arrow rather than the option for exactly that reason, and `RestoreOption` puts that one arrow back rather than every arrow carrying the same choice: the ones the player did not hit have to keep whatever the ball did to them.

The board is the plaque's shape and stands on the plaque's own lines (`ArkanoidSetup.MenuOverWordY`/`MenuOverScoreY`/`MenuOverArrowY` are `HallNameY`/`HallScoreY`/`HallArrowY`), so whatever keeps the champion's shadow out of their score keeps the words' shadow out of this score. The words are authored, one hittable block per letter as the title is, with **the space between GAME and OVER skipped** — a collider standing in the gap would bounce the ball off nothing — and they are drawn at `MenuOverCell`, which is not written down but worked out: GAME OVER is 53 cells where ARKANOID is 47, and the arrows stand where they stand on every board, so the word is sized to span exactly what the title spans. That keeps the 0.6 of a unit the 16:9 layout leaves between a word and the arrow beside it, and it keeps the title the largest lettering on the menu. The score is not authored — it is only known when a round ends — so `GameOverBoard.Show` builds that line at runtime through `BlockLine`, the same builder the hall's plaque uses, capped at the words' own cell so a number can never be the loudest thing on the screen. It is **white** (`MenuLabel`) where the words are masonry, which is the same split the plaque makes between a champion's name and their score: a name is lettering, a score is a readout, and what they are cut from is what tells them apart.

**Nothing on the menu leads back to this board**: a lost round is the only way in, which makes it the one screen that is arrived at rather than travelled to. Clearing the level still raises the old `RecordsPanel` overlay with its "YOU WIN!" line and its list — there is no board for a win, and `State.Won` is still a state with SPACE and ESC to leave it.

## The test bench

**Type BENCH on the menu and a room opens with nothing in it but the thing you are working on.** It exists because verifying a *look* used to mean playing a round to get to one: aiming the menu's ball at START, missing, and re-photographing — and because showing thirty-six blocks of one material meant editing `BuildLevel` and remembering to put it back. Both of those are what this replaces. It is reachable in five keystrokes from boot, in one `/input` script, with nothing to aim.

**It is a scratchpad, not a feature.** `TestBench.cs` is *expected* to be rewritten to suit whatever is being worked on — swap the grid for a single block, add a key that drives some new mechanic, tear out what is in the way. Changing it needs no more justification than "this is what I need to see", and nothing in the game reads it.

The room stands at **x 40**, two screen widths right of the playfield, mirroring the menu on its left; it is authored inactive (stage 98) and switched on by `GameManager.OpenBench`. It carries its own backdrop at the round's own gap and depth, so a block photographed here throws the shadow it would throw in play, and its own three `Border`s built at runtime, so a ball let loose ricochets off the frame as it does in a round.

| Key | Does |
|---|---|
| `Z` / `X` | previous / next material |
| `C` / `V` | previous / next shape |
| `N` / `M` | fewer / more blocks |
| `G` | spend a quarter of every block's hardness — parks on light crack, heavy crack, then breaks |
| `F` | rebuild at the same seed (which is also how damage is undone) |
| `R` | re-roll: a fresh draw of the material's variety |
| `B` | ball on/off |
| `P` | paddle on/off — the round's own paddle, with the round's own keys |
| `SPACE` | serve the ball off the paddle (and, held, thrust it) |
| `Q` or `ESC` | back to the menu |

Deliberately clear of **every key the paddle reads**, because the paddle can be standing here: it takes the arrows *and* `A`/`D` to move, `S` and DOWN to charge, and SPACE to thrust and serve. That is why damage is `G` and not the `D` it started as — `D` is the paddle's own "right". A control that means two things depending on what else is switched on is a control nobody can use. The on-screen readout names the shape, material, multiplier, count, seed and damage steps, because a screenshot that does not say what it is showing is a screenshot somebody has to come back and ask about.

Four things about it are load-bearing, and each was learned rather than designed:

- **`Q` exists because `ESC` cannot be driven from outside.** Uplink's `/input` delivers letters happily and never delivers escape at all — neither `"escape"` nor `"<Keyboard>/escape"`, measured twice with a log on the frame proving nothing arrived, while the call itself reports success. A bench whose only exit is a key no script can press traps whatever is driving it. `ESC` stays for hands; `Q` is the one for scripts.
- **A fixed seed, because the variety is random.** `Random.InitState(seed)` before laying the grid, so two screenshots of the same bench are comparable and a fresh set is asked for on purpose (`R`) rather than arriving unbidden. Note that Unity's `Random` is global: the bench reseeds it, so anything else drawing from it while the bench is open is drawing from a sequence the bench chose.
- **It must not be able to touch a round.** Blocks are spawned directly rather than through `SpawnBrick`, so nothing here is counted in `bricksLeft`; and `GameManager.OnBrickDestroyed` returns immediately while the state is `Bench`. That second line is the one that matters: `SetScore` writes straight through to the stored high score, so without it a block broken on a bench would raise the bar a real round has to clear, and could put a bench session in the hall of fame. Both facts live in `GameManager` rather than in `TestBench`, because a bench that could only be trusted as long as nobody edited it would not be worth having.
- **The paddle is copied out of the playfield, and `Instantiate`'s positioning overload is what makes that safe.** The paddle is authored scene content with no prefab, so `P` clones the round's own `Paddle` — which brings the thrust, the charge, the gauge, the exhaust and the crash along for free and can never drift from what a round does. But `Paddle.Awake` reads `homeX` off its own transform and clamps its travel to either side of it, so a copy created at the template's place and moved afterwards would spend the session trying to get back to the playfield. Handing the position to `Instantiate` puts it there before `Awake` runs. Measured: the bench paddle clamps at x 50.19 — its own room's edge, `homeX` 40 plus `xLimit` — which is the proof that `homeX` was read in the right room.
- **With a paddle standing, the ball waits for SPACE**, because that is the only shape in which the charge means anything: a push is paid to a ball at the moment it is *caught*, so a bench that launched for you would have nothing to catch. Without a paddle the ball launches itself, so an unattended bench keeps moving.
- **The readout carries the paddle's own numbers** — charge, drift — and `timeScale`. Those mechanics are invisible until they fire: a charge that is not filling looks exactly like a paddle standing still, and a frozen bench (the exit prompt sets `timeScale` to 0, and `timeScale` outlives the round that set it) looks exactly like a broken one.
- **The ball is served off a bare anchor** when there is no paddle, not placed by hand: `Ball.Launch` refuses to fire a ball that is not attached to something and quietly does nothing otherwise, which would leave the bench's one moving part sitting still. `AttachTo` measures its paddle's collider and renderer and falls back cleanly when there is neither, so no paddle is needed — and there is none here yet, which is the one obvious thing to add when a push, a charge or a jet trail is what needs looking at.

**Known problem, unsolved: entering the code is intermittent.** `BENCH` opens the bench perhaps half the time; the rest of the time the menu simply stays up. One real bug in it *has* been found and fixed — the letter loop advanced `benchProgress` and then fell through to a reset keyed on `anyKey.wasPressedThisFrame`, which is true of the very letter that had just advanced it, so every letter after the first undid itself. Instrumented logs then showed a clean progression (`progress=0..4`, `want=B..H`) ending in `OpenBench`, and the bench opening with its blocks and camera — so the fixed path demonstrably works. It still fails sometimes, and the cause of *that* is not known. When it happens, the bench is not open at all (`/scene` shows `/TestBench` inactive) rather than half-open, and re-running the same script usually works. Worth a fresh look with a log on `OpenBench` itself; do not assume the letters are being dropped, because that was the first guess and the logs refuted it.

`GameManager.BenchCode` reads the word on the menu only, where letters are otherwise unused — the arrows, SPACE and DOWN drive the paddle, ESC has nothing to leave, and name entry is a state of its own — so a word can be a door without taking a key away from anything. A wrong letter starts the word over, and the reset re-tests against position zero, since the wrong letter may be the first letter of a fresh attempt.

## Layout

- `Assets/Scripts/Paddle.cs` — keyboard paddle movement (`Keyboard.current`, arrows/A-D), clamped to `xLimit` either side of where the paddle was authored (`homeX`, taken in `Awake`) rather than either side of x 0, since the menu's paddle lives in the menu's room a screen's width to the left. `xLimit` itself is handed over by the room the paddle belongs to (`FitTo`) once it has measured its frame — both fields are the window's width now — and the paddle's own body is measured off its renderer, since the menu's paddle is a scaled-down copy of the round's. It also publishes `Drift` — which way it was travelling over the last frame, as −1, 0 or 1 — which is what `Ball.cs` reads to twist a bounce (see "The bounce can be twisted"). It is taken from whether the paddle actually travelled rather than from the key, so a paddle held against the edge of the field reports the standstill it is, and one coasting a boost off with nothing held reports the travel it still has. **SPACE is a thrust** (see "The paddle can be a rocket"): `drive`, the paddle's travel as a signed multiple of cruising speed, is spooled up to `BoostTopSpeed` while SPACE and an arrow are held together, coasts back to cruising when they are not, and is spent entirely on running out of field — where it also hands `ViewShake` a crash proportional to whatever it was carrying over cruising speed. `Exhaust` hangs the `JetTrail` off the paddle's trailing edge for as long as the drive is over cruising speed, measuring the nozzle, the flame and the embers off the paddle's own drawn body and paying out embers on a rate rather than one a frame. **DOWN winds up a push** (see "The push can be charged"): `Wind` fills `Charge` over `ChargeTime` and, the instant the key is not held, turns it into `ReleasedCharge`/`ReleasedAt` for the ball to collect — spent either way, whether or not there was a ball to take it, and lapsed by the paddle itself once `Ball.PunchWindow` has gone by so a charge let go of a rally ago can never turn up on an unrelated catch. `Waves` drives the `PowerWave` gauge under it every frame — charge or no charge, since a gauge just let go of has a flying-apart to finish — making it on the first charge and hiding it in `OnDisable`, because it is an unparented root and does not go away with the paddle the way a child would. The three mechanics never argue over a key: the arrows steer, SPACE is the thrust and the serve, DOWN is the charge.
- `Assets/Scripts/Ball.cs` — constant-speed ball with a `Damage` value (currently 1) read by bricks on impact. Kinematic and paddle-following while attached; dynamic after launch. **The first launch is a fixed 15° off vertical, to the right** (`LaunchAngle`), and the waiting ball sits at the point on the paddle that angle comes off rather than dead centre: the paddle's arcade bounce turns a hit's distance from the middle, as a fraction of the paddle's half-width, into the tangent of the angle the ball leaves at, so the rest offset is `tan(LaunchAngle) x` the paddle's measured half-width. The paddle is measured rather than assumed, so the menu's ball and the round's both serve the same way — as is the rest *height*, off the paddle's and the ball's own drawn half-heights plus `RestClearance`, since the menu's pair are a scaled-down copy and a fixed height would leave them floating apart. **Across** the paddle is measured by its *collider*, because the bounce measures the same thing and the two have to agree; **upward** by its *renderer*, because `Collider2D.bounds` leaves out `edgeRadius` and the paddle carries most of its height in exactly that, so a collider-measured height sits the ball a corner radius inside the paddle it is meant to rest on. `FixedUpdate` renormalizes velocity to `speed`, and **a ball that has been stopped dead is sent off again** rather than left alone (`PinnedSpeed`, aimed by `Steepen` at `StallEscapeAngle` away from the last surface touched): normalising a heading of nothing gives nothing back, so the bail-out this replaces made a pinned ball a permanently pinned one. Two faces meeting almost head on can stop it — an option arrow's notch, where its tail meets its body, is where it was seen, once refused arrows stopped shattering out from under the ball. Paddle bounces off the flat top override the physics reflection with an angle based on where the ball hit the paddle **plus the paddle's own `Drift` through `TwistReach` (0.35), clamped back into the ±1 the hit offset alone spans** (see "The bounce can be twisted"); hits on the paddle's rounded corners (tilted contact normal) keep the engine's reflection off the curve and are never twisted. The same hit **scuffs** the ball: `spin` takes the paddle's `Drift` on top of half of what the ball arrived with (`ImpactScrub`) and bleeds away over the seconds after (`SpinDecay`), read out as a visible roll (`RollSpeed`, written to `angularVelocity` every step so nothing else can turn the ball) and as a bend in the heading (`CurveRate`, applied by `Rotate` ahead of the `MinAngle` floor so that floor still wins), both signed by `SpinHandedness`. Every other contact nicks the spin instead (`BounceScrub`, `BounceNick`, `NickFloor`), the serve draws a nick of its own, and `MinSpin` holds the result off zero — which together are what keep a ball in play always turning. A catch also records **who caught the ball and when** (`puncher`, `caughtAt`), which is the ball's half of the push (see "The push can be charged"): `TakePush` reads the release the paddle was already holding — the window opens before the bounce only, and the frame or two it waits is the slop between the two clocks rather than a window a late release can land in — and turns charge × how early it was into `punch` — borrowed speed as a fraction of the ball's own, taken as the greater of what is there and what arrived so a second push is a top-up and not a stack. `Speed` (`speed × (1 + punch)`) is what every rule reads, so the angle floor, the pinned-ball test and the arcade bounce all get the boosted speed for free, and `FixedUpdate` bleeds `punch` away at `PunchDecay` and snaps it to nothing at `PunchFloor`. `Launch` and `AttachTo` clear it: a serve is the ball's own speed, always. The push is weight as well as pace: `Damage` is the authored damage times `SpeedMultiple`, and `Punch` spends the broken block's hardness out of that multiple and carries the ball straight on through the hole — back onto `wayIn`, the heading it took into the physics step — for as long as there is any of the push left. Past `WakeSpeed` (1.5× its own speed) `Wake` hangs a blue `JetTrail` off the ball's trailing edge, laid along the heading of the moment so it bends with the curve, sized off two fractions of the ball's own drawn diameter (`WakeBore` across, `WakeReach` along — a jet wider than it is measured long), broken rather than bent by every contact so a bounce dissolves one ribbon and starts another, and lengthening, thickening and brightening with what is left of the push — the bleed made visible, and the other end of the blue gauge the charge was wound on. The ball knows nothing about the menu's screens changing around it: it is solid to everything, always (see "The main menu screen" for the transparency that was tried here and taken back out). What it does know is **being lifted out of its plane**: `PushInFrontOf` is told the depth of a face that has come up underneath it, and `LateUpdate` puts the ball its own radius in front of that face and lets it drop back at `PlaneReturn` (2.5 a second) once nothing is asking any more. That is Z alone — the rally is 2D and owns X and Y, and the two never meet — and it is only ever written for a ball something has actually lifted, so a round's ball keeps the depth it was spawned at untouched. The plane itself is taken from the paddle the ball is served off, since a ball is only ever served off one.

  **The ball must never settle into a rally it cannot get out of**, which the full-width field made much easier to fall into — three guards, one per way it happens. `MinAngle` (20°) is the floor under every fixed step: it used to be a hair under 9°, which stopped the ball going *exactly* sideways but little else, since a rally that flat crossed the frame in three seconds while climbing a quarter of it and read as a stuck ball; at 20° a crossing gains most of the frame's height. The paddle's own bounce never comes off flatter than 45°, so the floor only ever catches a ball the walls and bricks have flattened. `escapeY` is which way that floor sends a flattened ball, and it is taken from **the last surface the ball touched** rather than being fixed: the old guard defaulted a zero vertical component to *upwards*, so a ball that flattened while lying against the top border was shoved back into it every step and skimmed along it for ever — the endless horizontal rally. `Steepen` always reads `escapeY` rather than the sign of what is left of the ball's own vertical component, because a reflection can leave that sign a hair either side of zero, and away from the last surface is always right. Finally `StallBounces` (3) counts consecutive bounces off *vertical* faces — a side border, or the side of a brick, which return the ball with its vertical component untouched — and aims the ball out at `StallEscapeAngle` (35°) on reaching that count; anything else struck is progress and starts the count again. That last one is a backstop: at `MinAngle` the ball crosses the field at most twice before meeting the top or the paddle, so reaching three means something is flattening it that we did not foresee.
- `Assets/Scripts/Brick.cs` — accumulates the ball's `Damage` per hit against its `Hardness`, which is the prefab's `baseHardness` (the shape) times its material's multiplier (see "A block is a shape and a material"). Both are floats' worth of arithmetic now: a hit is worth what the ball was carrying, so `TakeDamage` is public, takes a float, is called by the *ball* rather than out of a collision handler of its own, and answers whether the block was destroyed — which is what tells the ball whether to come off it or punch through (see "The push can be charged"). It destroys itself and reports its `Points` to `GameManager` once damage reaches hardness, spawning falling `Debris` fragments in the material's own colour as it goes — handed `GameManager.Catcher`, so the chunks a block throws are worth catching rather than only worth watching. `SetMaterial` points the instance at a shared material asset rather than tinting a white one per instance; `SetLook` then puts a per-block override on top of it through a `MaterialPropertyBlock` for a material that has variety authored (grain, its normal, the batch tint, its gloss, and the tiling worked out from `grainUvPerUnit` — see "A material varies from block to block"), which does cost the batching the shared assets were there to protect. An unbreakable material refuses damage outright, and a detonating one takes its neighbours with it in `Break`/`Detonate`, guarded by `broken` against a chain running back the way it came. While still alive it shows a crack overlay on the prefab's `Cracks` child renderer — light crack up to half damage, heavy crack beyond — from parallel arrays of sprite variants wired in by the editor setup script. On its first hit each block picks a random variant index plus random X/Y renderer flips, then keeps them, so cracks differ between blocks while escalating damage reads as the same crack spreading (each heavy sprite extends its light counterpart). **The crack overlay has not been looked at on the translucent and near-black materials yet** — Crystal, Waspaloy and Neutronium — where a sprite pasted on the face is likely to read badly.
- `Assets/Scripts/TestBench.cs` — the room that opens when BENCH is typed on the menu (see "The test bench"): a grid of one shape crossed with one material, laid from a fixed seed, with keys to cycle either, step damage a quarter of a hardness at a time, re-roll the variety, switch a ball on and leave. A scratchpad meant to be rewritten per task, and structurally unable to score: it spawns blocks directly rather than through `SpawnBrick`, and `GameManager.OnBrickDestroyed` refuses to pay anything while the bench is open. `Q` leaves as well as `ESC`, because `/input` cannot send escape.
- `Assets/Scripts/BlockVariety.cs` — how far one block of a material may drift from the next one beside it (see "A material varies from block to block"): the grains it may be moulded with, the sRGB band its batch colour is drawn from, the gloss that rides along with that roll, and the `hueJitter` that has to be a *fraction* of each channel rather than an amount added to it. `Roll` makes one casting — grain, tint, smoothness and a random offset into the tiled grain — and hands back a `BlockLook` for `Brick.SetLook` to put on through a `MaterialPropertyBlock`. A material with no entry is the ordinary case, not a failure: the block then wears its shared asset exactly as authored.
- `Assets/Scripts/BlockMaterial.cs` — what a block is made of: the `BlockMaterial` enum (whose order is the index into `GameManager.blockMaterials`, so entries may be appended but not reshuffled), the `BlockMaterialTraits` a material carries (its hardness multiplier, whether it detonates, and `Unbreakable` for the multiplier of zero that cannot be arithmetic), and the `BlockMaterials.Of` table holding the ladder. The looks that go with them are authored in `ArkanoidSetup.BlockMaterialLooks`, beside the rest of the project's assets.
- `Assets/Scripts/Debris.cs` — the rubble of a shattering brick, spawned by `Brick` at destruction (runtime-only, nothing to author in the editor). 6–9 small cube fragments share the brick's material, tinted with per-fragment brightness variation of the brick's color via `MaterialPropertyBlock`; the cube mesh is lifted once off a throwaway `CreatePrimitive` template. Fragments carry no physics components — they are moved by hand in `Update` (initial outward/upward pop, constant gravity, random tumble), so they can never disturb the 2D gameplay colliders. `Spawn` takes an optional `amount` multiplier on the fragment count for casters bigger than a brick, and `ClearAll` sweeps every fragment still in the air, for screen changes that would otherwise inherit the last screen's rubble.

  **A round's rubble is worth catching** (see "Rubble is worth catching" above), which splits a fragment's life in two. Handed a `catcher` paddle, a fragment lives until it is caught or has fallen past the field, keeps to the playing plane (its Z velocity is zeroed, since the catch is judged in X and Y like everything else in the rally and a chunk caught a body's depth in front of the paddle would be seen not touching it), and tests itself against the paddle every frame — the *drawn* body rather than the collider, both across and upward, because catching rubble is an eye's judgement rather than the rally's contact and `Collider2D.bounds` leaves out the `edgeRadius` the paddle carries most of its height in. The test is the paddle's face grown by the fragment's own half-size, *plus* the step it just took crossed against that face's top: rubble is at its fastest by the time it arrives, fast enough to be above the paddle one frame and below it the next. A caught chunk bursts into a `Ricochet` where it landed — it is worth score, so it has to be seen being caught — and reports to `GameManager.OnDebrisCaught`. Handed no catcher, a fragment is scenery exactly as it always was: it shrinks away over the last third of a 1.2–2 s life (the opaque Lit material can't alpha-fade) and dies below the playfield. That is what the menu's shattering slabs spawn, since there is no score on the menu screen to add to.
- `Assets/Scripts/GameManager.cs` — state machine (Menu/Ready/Playing/EnteringName/GameOver/Won, of which `GameOver` is only ever an `endState` — a lost round is handed to `ShowMenu(score)` and the menu's game over board takes it from there), score, lives, high score persisted via `PlayerPrefs` (`Arkanoid.HighScore`), runtime brick-grid construction — the demonstration board of every shape crossed with every material, one of each (see "A block is a shape and a material") — and the nine block material assets in `blockMaterials`. The high score is still tracked and persisted, but it is no longer displayed anywhere — it only decides whether a finished round earns a `RecordBook` entry, and the hall of fame is where the numbers are read. The HUD is hidden on the menu and shows score and lives during a round. `Catcher` hands the round's paddle to a shattering brick's rubble and `OnDebrisCaught` pays `DebrisPoints` for a chunk landed on it, both refusing anything but a round actually in play — the menu's slabs shatter through the same code, and rubble goes on falling over an end screen and over name entry, where a score being submitted to the hall of fame must not creep up underneath it. When a round ends above the record held at round start, switches to `EnteringName`: reads the player's name key by key off `Keyboard.current` in `Update` (backspace edits, ENTER submits, 12-char cap) and appends a `RecordBook` entry. It used to subscribe to `Keyboard.onTextInput`, which never delivered a character and left the field looking dead. Reading keys is also the right path for what the name is *for*: `BlockText`'s 5 × 7 font, which the hall of fame draws the name in, holds only the capitals and the digits, so a champion who typed "Alex" went up on the plaque as "A???" — only glyphs that font can draw can be typed now (A–Z, 0–9, and a space that may not lead). Pushes values into the `ScoreBoard` and drives the `RecordsPanel` and `MainMenuPanel`, and switches `playfieldObjects` (the round's room and its paddle) off for the menu and back on for a round. OnGUI is used for the "press SPACE to launch" banner, which serves the menu's ball as well as a round's, and for the quit confirmation. **ESC leaves a round for the menu, on confirmation**: the prompt sets `Time.timeScale` to 0 so a ball in flight can't be lost while the player reads the question, and swallows every key but its own (Y/ENTER leaves, N/ESC keeps playing). It is only offered from `Ready` and `Playing` — the menu has nothing to leave, name entry is one ENTER from being submitted, and the win overlay just goes back on ESC as it does on SPACE. Abandoning a round winds the stored high score back to `previousRecord`, because `SetScore` has been pushing the running score into it all round and a walked-out-on score must not raise the bar the next round has to beat. `ShowMenu` now calls `ClearRound` (ball, brick grid, the round's rubble and ricochet sparks, any name-entry subscription): 2D physics ignores Z, so a ball or a grid of bricks left standing behind the menu's backdrop would still be in the menu ball's way, unseen — which is the same reason `playfieldObjects` goes off. Freezing time is not enough on its own for the components that read the keyboard themselves, so `MainMenuPanel.Update` bails out on `GameManager.Paused` rather than launching the menu's ball behind the prompt; the paddle needs no such guard because it moves by `Time.deltaTime`. Boots into the `Menu` state and reacts to `MainMenuPanel.OptionChosen`, which only ever means "start a game" — the menu's hall of fame is a screen inside the menu now, and nothing here is involved in it; a lost round goes back to the menu of its own accord, onto the game over board, and a cleared one still waits there on SPACE or ESC. Every state transition stamps `transitionFrame` and `Update` returns early on that frame, because `MainMenuPanel` reads the same keyboard in the same frame in an undefined order — without it the ENTER that picks `START` would also launch the ball. Serialized references are wired by the editor setup script.
- `Assets/Scripts/ScoreBoard.cs` — display-only UGUI HUD component on the `ScoreBoard` canvas (Score / Lives), anchored to the screen's bottom corners since the field runs the full width. The readouts sit under a `Hud` child, so `SetVisible` can switch the whole HUD off for the menu screen without touching the records and main menu panels on the same canvas; `GameManager` pushes the values in and shows the HUD for a round. There is no high score readout.
- `Assets/Scripts/RecordBook.cs` — static store for the chronological record-holder list (date/name/score), persisted in `PlayerPrefs` (`Arkanoid.Records`) as JSON. `Clear` is for testing and nothing in the game calls it.
- `Assets/Editor/RecordsMenu.cs` — the **Arkanoid ▸ Clear Records** menu command, which empties the record book *and* deletes the stored high score. Both, because the high score is the bar a round has to clear to be asked for a name: an emptied book with the old bar still standing would never reach name entry to be tested.
- `Assets/Scripts/RecordsPanel.cs` — display-only overlay under the `ScoreBoard` canvas: name entry after a new record, then the hall-of-fame list. Authored inactive; `GameManager` shows/hides it. What is left of it is name entry and **a cleared level**: a lost round ends on the menu's own game over board now (see "The board a lost round ends on"), and the menu's hall of fame is scene content (`HallOfFame`) rather than this list.
- `Assets/Scripts/MainMenuPanel.cs` — the menu's three screens, which are scene content rather than UI (see "The main menu screen" above). The component sits on the `MenuScreen` root, so `Show`/`Hide` is just switching that subtree on and off. It reassembles the title and every option arrow, fits the arrows, the paddle's travel and the room's three `Border` walls to the frame the camera actually has (`FitToFrame`, re-run whenever the window changes size), parks the paddle back under the middle and re-attaches the ball on `Show`, launches on SPACE, and materialises the ball back on the paddle whenever it leaves the camera's frame — during a screen change as much as outside one. `MenuOption` (on each arrow) reports the arrow it is to it — the arrow rather than the choice, since which board it stands on is what says whether it may be picked (`BoardOf`) — and shatters only if the hit is taken; `CarryOut` then waits out the shatter and either raises `OptionChosen` for `GameManager` (START only), changes to another of the slider's boards, or walks the hall back to the record before the one on show. A change is `ScreenChange`'s two movements — one board out across the frame, the other up out of the fog in place — measured off where the boards were authored (`HomeOf`), since they are not all one screen apart, with `MenuSlider` holding only the resting layout. `Show` opens on the title board and `ShowGameOver` on the board a lost round ends on, reading out the score it ended with. `ResetScreens` puts every board back at its authored place, every collider back on and every renderer back to its own colour and its own shadow, since leaving the menu for a round stops a change wherever it had got to. First hit wins while `busy`, since the ball is still live throughout — it plays on across the change rather than being re-served after it.
- `Assets/Scripts/GameOverBoard.cs` — the board a lost round comes to rest on: the authored words GAME OVER over the score it ended with, which is built at runtime (`BlockLine`) since it is only known then. A screen of the menu like the other two, standing between them (see "The board a lost round ends on").
- `Assets/Scripts/BlockLine.cs` — a line of the menu's block lettering built while the game is running, for the words nobody could author: a champion's name and score, and a lost round's score. One hittable block per symbol, spaces skipped, and the meshes destroyed with their blocks — shared by `HallOfFame` and `GameOverBoard` so the two read as the same stone.
- `Assets/Scripts/ScreenChange.cs` — the motion a menu screen change is made of, the fog it comes out of, and `ScreenPiece`: the transforms one screen is made of, the places they rest, the colliders that say whether it is in the game and the colours its renderers wear when nothing is in front of them. Shared by the menu's two boards and by the hall's champions, so both changes are the same change (see "A screen change happens in depth").
- `Assets/Scripts/MenuFog.cs` — one bank of drifting haze, in the menu's fog or the round's: both rooms hang two of them, since they stand in the same weather. Owns a copy of the shared haze material (the scale and the offset are what tell the banks apart), sets the tiling it was authored with and walks the cloud's UVs across the sheet in `Update`, dropping whole tiles as it goes so the offset can't grow until it loses precision. Runtime-only motion over editor-authored geometry, as everything else here is.
- `Assets/Scripts/HallOfFame.cs` — the menu's hall of fame screen, two screens left of the title board: the top ten of `RecordBook` by score, one champion at a time, walked down the book rather than cycled, arrived at rather than swapped in (`Advance`, a coroutine the menu waits on: the champion being left travels out across the frame and the record before theirs rises out of the fog) — lines and the "previous record" arrow together, since they are one plaque arriving. Builds the name and score lines at runtime through `BlockLine` (they are player-typed names, so nothing can be authored) as one hittable block per symbol, and destroys each block with its mesh as it moves on; hides the "previous record" arrow when the plaque has reached the bottom of the book, or the book is empty. Putting knocked-out symbols back is `MainMenuPanel.RestoreBoard`'s business, not this component's — the plaque is mended as part of the board it stands on.
- `Assets/Scripts/BlockText.cs` — the 5 × 7 block font (whole alphabet and digits, `?` for anything else; `Blank` says which of its symbols have nothing solid in them, which is how a word's spaces come to be skipped wherever one is built as a block per symbol) and the solid geometry built from it: one box per horizontal run of solid cells, UVs offset by the piece's place in a word so masonry runs across separately-built letters. Also `BuildArrowMesh`/`ArrowOutline` for the option arrows, mesh and collider from one outline, and `Pocket`/`Engraving` for the recess each arrow's white lettering is seated in. In the runtime assembly because `HallOfFame` needs it, and shared from there by the editor setup script.
- `Assets/Scripts/Border.cs` — one of the three walls closing a room at the left, right and top edges of the frame, in the menu and in a round alike. Built and fitted at runtime by the room that owns it — `MainMenuPanel` or `Playfield` — since there is nothing to author: the frame is the window's. Invisible, and the place a ball hit turns into a `Ricochet`. The direction the sparks fly is taken from the wall's own side rather than the contact, so it is the same every time whichever part of the ball touched first. `Border.FrameExtents` is the shared measurement both rooms fit themselves to.
- `Assets/Scripts/Playfield.cs` — the round's room: fits its three borders, the backdrop's and the two fog banks' sizes (each at its own depth, so no edge is ever seen) and the paddle's travel to the camera's frame on `OnEnable` and on any window resize, and publishes `HalfWidth`/`HalfHeight` for `GameManager` to lay the brick grid out against. The backdrop and the banks are root scenery it references rather than children, so the round's fog stands while the room is switched off for the menu.
- `Assets/Scripts/JetTrail.cs` — the exhaust behind anything travelling faster than it has any right to: a boosted paddle (see "The paddle can be a rocket") and a ball carrying a push (see "The push can be charged"): a plume of swept-path streaks off its trailing edge plus a scatter of embers, cosmetic and runtime-only like `Ricochet` and `Debris` and built the same way (plain cubes off the shared stock mesh, moved by hand in `Update`, no physics components anywhere near the gameplay colliders, tinted per instance). What it is asked for is a length of flame rather than a lifetime — the ribbon is the ground the nozzle covers while a piece lives, so the lifetime is worked out from `length / speed` and the flame is the same flame at any speed and any frame rate. It is measured in a `bore` across and a `reach` along, two units rather than one so that a caller can ask for a jet wider than it is long; the paddle passes its height for both and the ball two different fractions of its diameter. Every size is a fraction of the drawn body of whatever is burning, so the menu's smaller paddle gets a smaller rocket and the menu's smaller ball a smaller wake. What tells the two trails apart is the `Blaze` a caller hands it and nothing else — `Rocket`'s chemical orange against `Plasma`'s blue — and `GlowTop` is how strength buys brightness on top of length and thickness, by multiplying that palette rather than lerping it towards a white it must not reach. `ClearAll` sweeps it at a screen change, as `Debris.ClearAll` and `Ricochet.ClearAll` do.
- `Assets/Scripts/PowerWave.cs` — the gauge showing the charge on a paddle winding up a push (see "The push can be charged"): three waves standing under it, each smaller and brighter than the last, filling one after another so that the number standing full is the charge. Cosmetic and runtime-only like `JetTrail`, `Ricochet` and `Debris`, and built the same way — plain cubes off the shared stock mesh, moved by hand, no physics components, tinted every frame. `Attach` makes one for a paddle, `Tick` is driven by that paddle every frame and does the whole of the drawing (`Draw`, whose one interesting line is `fill = clamp01(charge × Waves − i)`), `Spend` starts the flying-apart a release plays, and `Hide` is what the paddle calls when it goes away. It grows downwards where the exhaust streams behind and is cold where the exhaust is hot, so the two mechanics on the one paddle can never be read as each other. Unparented rather than a child of the paddle, because the paddle's transform is scaled on the menu screen and every size here is a world size. `ClearAll` sweeps it at a screen change, as the others' do.
- `Assets/Scripts/ViewShake.cs` — the knock to the whole picture a paddle delivers by crashing into the edge of the frame under boost (see "The paddle can be a rocket"): a decaying wobble added to `Camera.main`'s position and taken back off again, runtime-only and added to the camera by the first knock rather than authored on it. It reads the view's rest position back out of the transform each frame instead of remembering it, so `GameManager`'s own writes to the camera — the travel from the menu into a round — are never fought over, and it switches itself off once a knock is spent, having put the view back exactly where it found it.
- `Assets/Scripts/Ricochet.cs` — the burst where a ball strikes a border, on the menu or in a round, and where a chunk of rubble lands on the paddle: a streak lying along the wall that stretches out and thins away, plus a scatter of sparks fanned about the normal. Cosmetic and runtime-only, moved by hand in `Update` for the same reason `Debris` is — no physics components near the gameplay colliders — but short and small where debris is slow and heavy. Spawned a little way inside the frame, since a burst centred on the screen's edge would spend half of itself outside it, and tinted up off the ball's own material to a pale flame colour. `ClearAll` sweeps the fragments at a screen change, as `Debris.ClearAll` does.
- `Assets/Scripts/MenuOption.cs` — one option arrow; a ball collision is the entire interaction. It shatters into `Debris` and switches itself off only when `MainMenuPanel.OnOptionHit` takes the hit as the choice — it hands the panel *itself* rather than the choice it carries, since HALL OF FAME and MENU each stand on two boards and where an arrow stands is what says whether it is up; a refused hit leaves it standing, because an arrow the player can't get back is a screen they can't leave. Its lettering is a white `ArrowInlay` block seated in a pocket in its face, so a shattered arrow throws rubble in both colours.
- `Assets/Scripts/MenuTitleBlock.cs` — one block of the menu's lettering, whether a letter of the title or a symbol of the hall of fame's plaque; a ball collision shatters it into `Debris` and switches it off.
- `Assets/Editor/ArkanoidSetup.cs` — `[InitializeOnLoadMethod]` script that generates the sprite textures (`Assets/Sprites`), configures their importers, creates the bouncy `PhysicsMaterial2D` (`Assets/Physics`) and the `Ball`/`Brick` prefabs (`Assets/Prefabs`), then authors the camera, the playfield room, the paddle and a wired `GameManager` into the scene, and finally the `ScoreBoard` screen-space canvas. Editor-authored content stays inspectable without Play mode; only the ball and bricks exist at runtime.

Stages 9 and 10 authored the menu as a UGUI panel and are gone now that the menu is scene content; the numbering after them was left alone rather than shuffled. Stages 40 and 50 are the ones the menu keeps coming back to: stage 40 rebuilds the block meshes whenever a new one is missing (which is how the arrows arrived and the title letters were rewritten at their smaller cell, and where the labelled slabs it all replaced were deleted), and stage 50 throws the whole `MenuScreen` away and re-runs stage 42's builder whenever its shape is behind that builder — first when the UGUI option list became a playable screen, then when the screen became two screens on a slider. Both guards are written against the *current* end state, so they stay quiet once the scene matches and are correct on a from-scratch build. **Stage 70 is the standing repair for what stage 40 costs, and it is the one stage that does not stand where its number says.** Writing a mesh asset over one already standing in the scene destroys the object the scene was pointing at, and the reference goes null rather than following the new one into the same file — so a re-run of stage 40 blanks whatever it rewrote. It went unnoticed for as long as stage 50 followed it and authored the whole screen afresh; the first re-run that didn't (engraving the arrows, which changed no transform stage 50 keys on) left ARKANOID missing from the middle of its own menu. Stage 70 sits **directly behind stage 40 and ahead of every stage that saves**, because a save taken while a reference is null writes the damage to disk, which is exactly how the missing title came to be committed. It also saves itself on the same tick rather than through a paired stage: a disk-side gate is the one thing that cannot be written safely for it, since a renderer holding no mesh is a permanent and legitimate feature of this scene (the hall's two champion lines are anchors and draw nothing), so a gate on one would queue a save on every reload for ever. Stage 40 for its part now rebuilds the title's letters, the game over board's letters and the arrows independently, so retuning one never makes the others pay; and the repair's list of letters is `MenuLetters`, which pairs each block standing under an authored word with the mesh it should be drawing — walked alongside the characters that *have* a mesh rather than by child index, since GAME OVER's space has none. **Stages 77 and 78 are the other pair that stands out of its numbered place**, ahead of stage 40 rather than at the end: they rename the hall's arrow from `OptionNext` to `OptionPrev` (it fetches the record before the one on the plaque now, rather than the next name in a cycle) and save that. The order is what makes it safe — stage 40 is about to write the arrow's re-lettered `MenuArrowPrev`/`MenuInlayPrev` meshes and delete the `MenuArrowNext`/`MenuInlayNext` ones it holds, and stage 70 puts those back by looking the object up under its path, so a rename left until afterwards would move the object out from under that path in exactly the window where its mesh reference is null. The re-lettering itself is why the new meshes went to new paths: PREV is the same banner at the same width as NEXT, so nothing stage 40's size checks measure would have told them anything had changed — a missing file does. Stages 58, 60 and 62 are the same kind of small edit in place: standing the menu's backdrop back off the plane so a screen could arrive between the two, spacing the plaque's lines out for the longer shadow that came with it, and then — once an arriving screen rose out of the fog in place and needed no room at all — bringing the backdrop back in against the plane as the fog's far side, matting its material and closing the plaque's lines up again. Stage 62 reverses stages 58 and 60 rather than editing them, which is why their guards, written against a `MenuBackdropZ` that has since moved, are worth reading twice: each is only ever true of a scene that has not had the stage after it. Stages 68 and 72 are more edits in place, and between them they moved the lettering from in front of a banner's face to inside it: 68 points each of the four option arrows at the mesh carrying its pocket and deletes the `ArrowLabel` child that used to stand proud of it, and 72 seats the white `ArrowInlay` blocks down in that pocket. Stages 79–82 give the ball something to show its roll with and let it turn: a texture of four panels, imported without mipmaps (a 64 px texture on a ball drawn a dozen-odd pixels across would be handed an averaged-out mip level and the panels would be flat grey — the exact failure of drawing something on a ball to be seen), put on `Ball.mat` as its base map, and the prefab's `Rigidbody2D` rotation constraint lifted. None of them touches the scene, so none needs a paired save: the menu's ball is a prefab instance that overrides nothing here and comes along for free. Stage 79's guard is the file's absence, so retuning the panels means deleting the PNG — keeping its `.meta`, since a new guid would break the material's reference to it — and letting the stage write it again; how dark the dark panel is went 0.5 → 0.22 → 0.38 that way, the first reading as shading on a sphere rather than a mark on one and the second sinking the ball's dark half into the backdrop it has to be tracked across. Stage 74 stands the round's room in the menu's fog — backdrop in to the fog wall wearing the shared fog material, two banks of haze hung as root scenery and wired into `Playfield` — and stage 76 paces the menu's ball to the round's speed; 76's guard is the serialized speed itself, an in-memory fact, so like stage 70 it saves on its own tick rather than through a disk-side gate, which a bare float in the scene file could never anchor. Stage 85 is a one-line follow-up to them, giving that board's score line the plaque's own white `MenuLabel` where stage 83 cut it from the title's masonry; its guard is the material the line is wearing, an in-memory fact, so it saves on its own tick rather than through a paired stage, since a disk-side gate would have to key on a material guid the arrows' lettering carries all over this scene. **Stages 86–90 split a block's hardness into its shape and its material.** 86 writes the nine block materials; 87 wires them into the `GameManager`; 88 sets `baseHardness` to 1 on the half-width and round prefabs (the full-slot ones keep the field's own default of 2); 89 rebuilds `BrickRounded.asset` at the wider corner radius and 90 puts that mesh back on the prefab and widens its collider to match. **Stage 87 is a standing repair, not a one-off wiring**, and for exactly the reason stage 70 is: rewriting a material asset over one already referenced destroys the object the scene was pointing at, so the slot goes null rather than following the new material into the same file — and retuning a block's look means precisely that rewrite. Its guard therefore reads the array's *contents* rather than merely its length, which a first version got wrong: retuning the materials left a whole grid wearing the prefabs' old white `Brick.mat` while nine perfectly good materials sat beside it, and the length-only guard stayed quiet throughout. Stages 89 and 90 are the same pairing one step down — 89 destroys the prefab's mesh reference and 90 is what puts it back — and neither touches the scene, so neither needs a save. **Retuning a block's look means deleting the `.mat` files and letting stage 86 write them again**, keeping their `.meta` files so the guids the scene references survive, the same way stage 79's ball texture is retuned; stage 87 then repairs whatever that rewrite broke. Stage 89's guard measures the corner radius off the mesh's own vertices (the outline's topmost sit at x = ±(width / 2 − radius)) rather than trusting a file to be absent, since the mesh has always been written to the same path.

**Stages 91–97 give Polymer a surface** (see "A material varies from block to block"). 91 writes the three grains as six PNGs — a near-white albedo and a normal map each; 92 reimports the three normals *as* normal maps, which is the one failure in the set that shows up as a wrong hue rather than as flat geometry (a tangent-space map left on the default importer type is lit as though the surface were painted lilac); 93 puts the first grain and its normal on the shared `BlockPolymer.mat` and turns the shader's `_NORMALMAP` keyword on — a keyword is a fact about the material and cannot be overridden per instance, so **every per-block grain in the game rides on that one call**; 94 writes each block prefab's `grainUvPerUnit`; 95 wires Polymer's `BlockVariety` into the `GameManager`. **Stage 95 is a standing repair for the same reason stages 70 and 87 are** — stage 91 rewrites texture assets, and a rewrite over a path already referenced destroys the object the scene was pointing at, so those slots go null rather than following the new PNG into the same file. Its guard therefore reads the entry's *contents*, both texture arrays element by element, because a list of the right length with a null grain in it is exactly what that failure leaves behind and `VarietyOf` would then quietly hand every Polymer block the shared asset with nothing to say anything had been lost. It is an in-memory fact, so like 70, 76, 85 and 87 it saves on its own tick rather than through a paired disk-gated stage: a texture guid in the scene file says nothing about which slot is holding it.

**Stages 98–100 build the test bench's room** (see "The test bench"): 98 authors the room and its backdrop *inactive* and also repairs a room standing at the wrong X, 99 saves it, 100 wires the four shape prefabs and the ball onto it and hands the room to the `GameManager`. Only the room and its backdrop are authored; everything the bench shows is spawned at runtime, which is the point — the arrangement is meant to be rewritten per task, and a scratchpad whose contents lived in the scene file would put every experiment into a scene diff.

**Stage 53 is gone, and how it died is the useful part.** It was the disk-gated save for stage 52, gated on the scene file still containing `m_LocalPosition: {x: 20, y: 0, z: 0}` — the hall of fame's old place. That is a gate on *residual damage*, and such a gate is only ever as good as its pattern not turning up in the scene for some other perfectly good reason. It did: the bench's room was first stood one screen width right of the playfield, which is exactly x 20. The gate went true for ever, and because a stage `return`s after queueing its save, **every stage after it stopped running** — which is what makes this class of bug expensive rather than merely noisy, and it presented as "my new stage never fires". Stage 52's own guard is an in-memory fact (a transform is where it is), so it now registers the deferred save itself, the way stages 70, 76, 85, 87 and 95 do, and needs no disk gate at all. The bench moved to x 40 as well, since standing where no gate is looking costs nothing. **Before writing a gate on residual state, grep the scene for the pattern** — and prefer the new state's signature, or no disk gate at all where the stage's own guard is reliable.

Stages 96 and 97 are the pairing one step down, and they exist because of the grain rather than alongside it: 96 writes `BrickBox.asset` and `HalfBrickBox.asset` (`BuildWorldUvBoxMesh` — unit-size geometry with the block's proportions baked into the UVs, so a UV unit is a world unit on all six faces) and 97 puts them on the two prefabs, leaving the transform scale exactly as it was. Neither touches the scene, so neither needs a save. The winding is got right for all six faces by taking the second in-plane axis as `Cross(normal, u)` rather than by hand-checking six sign conventions — and it has to be *right*, because a box wound the wrong way round is not a subtle fault: back-face culling throws away the near face, so what is drawn is the far face and the inside of the box, and the block reads as a stepped, hollow, plainly corrupt shape. Stage 96's guard measures that winding off the mesh's own first triangle (every face is wound the same way, so the whole box is either right or inverted) rather than asking whether the file exists, for the same reason stage 89's measures the corner radius: the path is the one it has always been written to, and a file-existence guard could never repair a mesh that is present and wrong.

**Two traps were paid for here, and the second is the one worth remembering.**

- **Object identity is the wrong question to guard a mesh reference with.** `filter.sharedMesh != wanted` can be true of a prefab that is already pointing at exactly the right asset, because the mesh a prefab resolves to is not guaranteed to be the same instance `LoadAssetAtPath` hands back — so the repair ran, and ran again on every reload, and never settled. `BlockBoxMeshDiffers` compares `AssetDatabase.GetAssetPath(sharedMesh)` against the wanted path instead, which is the durable fact and also the question the stage actually has.
- **`SaveAsPrefabAsset` writes the file; it does not refresh the Editor's imported copy of it.** This is the nastier one, because every piece of evidence short of running the game says the repair worked: the guid lands correctly in the `.prefab`, `git diff` reads as a clean re-point, and `PrefabUtility.LoadPrefabContents` hands back a root whose `sharedMesh` is the right mesh. But `AssetDatabase.LoadAssetAtPath<GameObject>` on that same prefab returned a copy whose `sharedMesh` was **null** — it had been imported during the window in which stage 96 had just destroyed the mesh — and `Instantiate` hands out that imported copy, so every block in the round came up with no mesh at all. A plain `AssetDatabase.Refresh`, a domain reload, re-opening the scene, and even deleting the mesh assets so they reimported under fresh guids all left it standing. `AssetDatabase.ImportAsset(path, ImportAssetOptions.ForceUpdate)` after the save is what clears it. **The diagnostic that found it is worth reusing**: log `sharedMesh` at three points — off the prefab contents before the set, after the set, and off `LoadAssetAtPath` after the save. `before=BrickBox … after-save=NULL` names the fault exactly, where the `.prefab` file and the scene YAML both look perfect.

Stages 83 and 84 are the newest edit in place, and the largest since stage 56: 83 builds the board a lost round ends on into the slider and moves the hall of fame a screen further out to make the room for it, 84 saves that, gated on the scene file not yet naming `MenuOver` — the new state's own signature rather than the hall's old place, which is a position the slider's boards could legitimately stand at again. Stage 42's builder authors the board directly, so a from-scratch run never reaches stage 83 at all, and the board's arrows reuse the hall's and the title board's own banner meshes, so stage 40 had only its letters to add. Stages 52 and 54 are the exception to that habit: swapping the hall of fame to the board's left, and moving the whole menu into a room of its own left of the playfield (with its backdrop pulled in to that room's width), each moved a transform or two and nothing else, so they edit in place rather than paying for another wholesale rebuild. Stage 56 is the same kind of edit at a larger scale: it deletes the three masonry walls, puts a `Playfield` component on the room they hung off, re-anchors the four HUD texts to the bottom of the canvas and scales the menu's paddle and ball — all scene edits, so they fit in one pass, since only asset writes have to be read back on a later reload. Stages 17–27 still build, texture and re-mesh those walls on a from-scratch run and stage 56 still takes them out afterwards: keeping the intermediate valid is cheaper than unpicking ten stages, and each of their guards already no-ops once the walls are gone. Stage 24's did not — an "asset guid not in the scene yet" gate goes true again the moment the walls that referenced it are deleted — so it now also requires a wall to still be standing. The crack textures are generated from a per-variant seeded `System.Random` walk (`Assets/Sprites/CrackLight0-3.png` / `CrackHeavy0-3.png`), so a from-scratch rebuild reproduces the same set. Every stage that edits the scene only marks it dirty; a paired follow-up stage saves it from a self-removing `EditorApplication.update` handler on the next editor tick, to avoid the modal dialog described below. This works around `AssetDatabase` writes not being reliably readable back via `LoadAssetAtPath` within the same call. If you touch this file, preserve the one-stage-per-reload structure. The scene stage is guarded by `GameObject.Find("GameManager")`, so once set up the whole method is a no-op.

Folder existence is checked on disk (`System.IO.Directory`) rather than via `AssetDatabase.IsValidFolder`, which can go stale in driven sessions.

**Caution:** the scene stage calls `EditorSceneManager.SaveScene` from `[InitializeOnLoadMethod]`; in one session Unity then detected the open scene's file change and raised a *modal* "scene changed on disk" dialog that blocked the Editor main thread (and the Uplink bridge) until dismissed by hand. Either button is safe (disk and memory content are identical), but be aware programmatic scene saves can trigger it.
